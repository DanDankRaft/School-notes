# Dworkin's Auction
- He takes an unorthodox approach on the market.
- Typically, market enthusiasts don't care about egalitarianism, and instead highlight the overall material prosperity and human choice they bring.
	- IE, maximization and individual responsibility at the expense of equal respect.
- Egalitarians instead viewed markets as the progenitor of inequalities.
- Marx talks about these two spheres of the market in *Das Kapital*:
	- "The noisy sphere of circulation" - the surface level where it seems like 
	- "The hidden bode of production" - subordination and exploitation

- Dworkin looks at markets as a tool for egalitarianism.
- Why does Dworkin like markets so much?
	- They tell us the true value of goods in a decentralized way!
- It lets people create choices, but because those choices have a price, they also come with individual responsibility.

- By wagering a number of clams on a certain good, you reveal your own preferences.
- The responsibility for your choices and preferences is fulfilled through the price. You're forced to be held responsible for expensive wants and needs by paying more for them!

- Initial thoughts about the market:
	- What if you're disabled? The market doesn't care about why you want/need something, or whether it's fair to hold you responsible for that want/need. It just cares how much you want/need it.
	- The market is unconcerned about people's wants and needs beyond what it can provide.
	- What about addictive substances? Externalities.
- The market's criterion is willingness and ability to pay. In his story about the island, that's fine.

- But in our world, where we don't start with equal clamshells, it quickly becomes a highly problematic criterion.
- Markets set in motion processes with negative systematic outcomes and externalities.

- Thoughts about the envy test:
	- Evan: it looks like a reinvention of Pareto Optimal, even though that's exactly what Dworkin wants to avoid.
	- The point of the envy test is to make sure nobody has the grounds to complain that they couldn't get someone else's bundle.

## What happens after some time passes
- People do things with their bundles in the world.
- Some of them do things that are very economically productive, others do not.
- Do you equalize resources again?
	- A pattern approach - you're committed to some set of social outcomes as an equitable version of society. The moment society starts to diverge from that equal status, is the moment that trouble starts.
	- Dworkin says no! Equality must be *sensitive to ambition* (life plans) as opposed to outcomes.
		- It's a violation of personal responsibility! It makes your choices meaningless!

- Over what timeframe do we equalize between people? Bracha's answer: over lifetimes.
	- When you compare the total amount of resources someone has all across their life time, we should make sure two people are the same. Someone's "bundle of goods" is something you measure as something that totals over their lifetime. The days you spent in leisure drinking champagne count as part of your bundle. That's something you get to choose as part of your initial auction.

- On the other side, Dworkin also denies he's a starting-gate theorist. He doesn't just care about equality at one point in time, he cares about equality from start to finish.
	- He wants to compensate for brute luck - "health, disability, talents."
	- Maybe you get hit by lightning, get sick, are born with a disability.
	- This is where he introduces internal resources, and says we need to equalize when considering both internal and external resources when equalizing.
- It is not justified to hold people responsible for those differences.

- Claude and Adrien both choose the life of industrious farmers at the initial auction. But it turns out that Claude is terrible at growing vegetables and Adrian is great at it. Should we compensate Claude for this?

- What non-choices do we choose to compensate? Brute luck vs. Option luck.
	- Option luck - when you make some sort of gamble and it does/doesnt pay off. For example, when you pick a more risky crop and it doesn't yield many crops.
- Insurance converts any brute luck situation into an option luck situation. If you end up in a bad brute luck situation but haven't bought insurance, it's no longer brute luck, but it's now option luck.
- Problem: what if you can't afford insurance? We don't live in a world where everyone can afford insurance in an equal and fair basis.
- Solution: hypothetical private insurance premiums. *Assuming that you pay a fair and ethical private insurance premium and don't know your own risk profile, how much would you pay in insurance?* And then everyone pays as much as they want to, and gets compensation proportional to your payments.

- The insurance scheme uses the market to force out information.


- The mistake in the insurance example:
	- "we should cap what you get via a social safety net by means of what you choose to pay" - fallacy of thinking that internal personal choices are the same as social choices.
	- This is a mistake that liberal thinkers make all the time! Because they think of everything in such individualized terms!

# Anderson reading

### Her critique of luck egalitarianism
- There isn't really a difference between brute luck and option luck.
- Most choices are a blend of the two.
- Unless we're being very paternalistic (and offensive), it's disrespectful to prevent or dissuade people from making certain choices 
- She doesn't like that we compensate the downtrodden with pity rather than compassion.
	- Pity v. compassion: 


- The problem is option luck is that you reduce their value to the decisions they've made.
- The market imposes an objective standard on people for how they should live their life.
	- Dworkin's assertion - that it's simply a product of "nature" is wrong. The market exists within a social structure created by humans. It's still some objective standard that humans can change!
- There's a difference between personal choices within a system that dictate the outcome, and the system that this choice is made within.

- The material view of social justice (what do you actually get - welfare, resources, capabilities) is the most important part
- Social equality and symbolic dignity matters too.
