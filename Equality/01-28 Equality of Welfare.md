# Welfarism
- A series of views built on the assumption that the important thing in human life from which we build moral and political theories is *welfare*.
- What is welfare?
	- A **subjective** human state of some kind. Each person's level of welfare is determined by their own lives. Different people have different needs, preferences, etc.
	- Welfare is **Commensurable** - you can measure people's welfare and put it on a scale, comparing different people's welfare in life.
- Many welfarists focus on *maximization* - increase welfare as much as possible.
- Utilitarians are welfarists that argue that welfare is a psychological state - happiness.
- Different answers for what is welfare - individual preferences, wealth.
	- How is wealth subjective? Wealth by willingness and ability to pay. Maximize the number of dollars amounts that a person is willing and able to pay for something. IE, if one person needs very little money to have most of what they are willing to pay for, and another needs much more, they can have unequal money and equal wealth.

# Why is Welfare Attractive?
- Welfare intuitively feels like what people really want.
- It's holistic - it allows us to look at a person's life as a whole instead of just one metric.
- Subjectivity is really attractive - it accounts for differences between people's needs and wants in a way that other theories of equality don't.
	- Subjectivity is really congruent with *equal respect*. It respects people's preferences for the life they want to live. 
# Dworkin's critique of Welfarism
- One big theme: some interests cannot be put on the same scale as others. Some preferences are *illegitimate*
	- Even if some people gain welfare from fulfilling political interests like instituting slavery, we don't want to consider that.
	- Why? Those preferences violate *equal concern*. Instituting slavery means we are no longer concerned about the welfare of slaves. But the whole reason we care about equality of welfare *is* equal concern. It's incoherent to reward a preference that some people desire to be subordinating or subordinate.
	- **It turns out, that we must filter out some things that people really care about. It goes against the fundamental appeal of equality of welfare - which is rewarding subjectivity!!**
- Examples of preferences we have to filter out:
	- political preferences: preferences about what theories of justice and organization of society we should follow. We have to decide on which political preferences we're going to agree on before we begin with our attempt at equalization.
	- impersonal preferences: choices that go beyond what directly affects you. Should we have more parks or a highways? Should we invest in going to the moon? We settle those preferences through a *political process*.
- Equality of welfare only applies to *personal preferences.*

- Another big theme: there's a difference between local success and global success. IE, how successful you are within the life you currently vs how successful you are at living the life you want to live.
	- Ex: one person cares about having a stable but successful 9-5 job. But other people care about having a sporadic but ambitious career. The latter type of person would be 
	- Part of the problem: it doesn't make sense to equalize overall success as a criterion of justice when different people care about this to a different amount.
	- Different people's preferences are grounded in different, more fundamental viewpoints about life. Those differences are incomparable.
	- Response: equalize within those more fundamental viewpoints. Dworkin says that's not workable. How do you objectively compare someone's success as a corporate lawyer with another's success as a sporadic artist?
	- **Even if we focus on the kind of welfare that we can equalize, it turns out that different people put different kinds of value on different things in life, so we can't really equalize those.**

- Another big theme: expensive taste. Some preferences take more resources to satisfy than others!
	- Ex: Jackie needs a fancy new Ferrari to get a basic amount of welfare, and only Bracha needs a 1990s Subaru. Equalizing welfare between the two of them would mean giving Jackie way more resources.
	- This intuitively feels wrong. Why?
	- There's something that feels icky about rewarding certain expensive taste.
	- To be clear - you can have expensive taste for noble things. Like being on the cutting edge of medical research.
	- Resources are finite. By having expensive taste, and you equalize for success, you take away opportunity cost from others in order to reach success. You take away resources from others. You impose the cost of tastes and preferences on others - violating equal respect.
	- Dworkin thinks: grow up. be a responsible adult. don't make others subsidize your expensive taste.
# Dworkin's Alternative: Equality of Resources
- What is a resource? A *means* to get other things. Like money. And other stuff. But mostly money.
- Equality of Welfare is about equalizing the ends. Equality of Resources is about equalizing the means. And then you're personally responsible for what you do with those resources. If you prefer Ferraris over Subarus, you'll just get less Ferraris.
- How this is a response to the "old egalitarians:" it factors personal responsibility into his thinking from step 1.


# Critiques of Dworkin's analysis
- Catherine - Dworkin doesn't really distinguish between *wants and needs*. What if someone needs expensive medication? How about expensive needs? Rather than expensive tastes?
- **Expensive Needs**: you need more resources to get to the same level as others in terms of physical or cognitive ability.
- Equality of resources feels unsatisfactory because it doesn't really address people's equal needs.
- It comes down to personal responsibility - someone with expensive taste *chooses* to be into Ferraris, someone with a handicap doesn't choose that.
- The problem - the difference between needs and wants is not clear. Some people got a handicap because of their past life choices. Some people don't need to address their handicap in order to be successful in life. Is addressing that handicap now a want? or a need?'
