# Legislative Standing
# Raines v. Byrd
521 U.S. 881 (1997)
> [!error] No standing for individual former and current members of congress unless they suffer a concrete, personalized injury - like having their votes nullified
## Background
+ Congress passed a bill giving the president a line item veto
+ One of the provisions in the bill allows individuals members of congress to sue in the federal courts over the constitutionality of this bill.
+ Plaintiffs are several members of congress, all opposing this bill, who brought this action before SCOTUS.
	+ What's the injury here - it *dilutes* the power of individual legislators. It prevents them from adding government pork to presidential priorities.
## Question
- Do members of Congress have a concrete injury when a law deprives them of some powers?
## Holding (Rehnquist)
- Not really.
- Some reasons (potentially dicta):
	- this wasn't the legislature as a whole, or enough legislators to even make a majority!
	- Institutional injury doesn't work - it must be a private injury
- Distinguishing from *Coleman* (a case where state legislatures sued bc they thought some tie-breaker vote from the lieutenant governor was unconstitutional; Article V says that only the state legislatures can ratify amendments):
	- In *Coleman*, the **votes of the individual legislators were effectively nullified. They suffered a personal injury** - how is this not an institutional injury?
	- They had enough votes for the resolution to pass their way, they didn't have to have the lieutenant governor come in to break the tie.
		- Something weird here - they effectively say that the legislators only had standing in *Coleman* bc they were right on the merits.
	- Here, the plaintiffs merely allege that the law makes their future votes in Congress less effective. That's not the same thing!
## Souter concurrence
- This is about separation of powers. It'd be better if we got to be more impartial by having some private party bring this claim to us.
## Dissents
- Stevens - this is a constitutional right towards individuals (the legislators) that's being violated!
- Breyer:
	- This is not distinguishable from *Coleman*
	- The jurisdictional statute limits us to only constitutional considerations here!
	- "The constitution does not draw an absolute line between disputes involving a 'personal' harm and those involving an 'official' harm"

# Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Ind. Redistricting Commission
576 U.S. 787 (2015)
> [!done] Yes standing for the legislature as a whole when its nullifying its future votes over something
## Background
- Arizona voters passed a referendum creating an independent redistricting commission.
- The legislature **as an institution** sued, saying that they reserved this right under the Elections Clause (Article I, Section 4, Clause 1).
### Legal Question
- Is this distinguishable from *Raines*? If so, how?
## Holding
- YES. This party has standing.
- One reason - they're an institutional plaintiff representing legislature as a whole, not just individual legislators.
- Another reason - this is analogous to *Coleman*: an act nullifying the future votes of the legislature until there's a new constitutional amendment 

- Dissent - court should not involve itself in disputes between legislature and executive of the same government.
- Another possibility - SCOTUS is more reluctant to get involved in state government disputes.
- Interesting note - Ginsburg argued that legislature had standing but that they should lose on the merits. Scalia argued that legislature had no standing but that they should win on the merits.
- Scalia is the most against legislative standing; Alito is the most in favor of legislative standing.
# Virginia House of Delegates v. Bethune-Hill
587 U.S. ___ (2019)
> [!error] No standing for a legislature as a *defendant* when plaintiff is suing the executive over a constitutional violation
## Background
- Voters sued Virginia's executive branch after the legislature drew new state house maps, for being a racial gerrymander.
- After losing in a lower court, the state AG announced that he would not appeal the case further. The Virginia House of Delegates then petitioned it themselves.
> [!info] Every time a party appeals a case, it has to show standing.
## Legal Question
- Can they do that? Appeal a case where they're injured by a decision but were not a party?
- Do they have standing
## Holding
- NO. They cannot appeal
- Under VA's constitution, the legislature doesn't have the authority to represent the state here - it's only the AG's job.
- Also, the House of Delegates is suing by itself - not the legislature as a whole! You need both houses!
	- This is what distinguishes this case from *Coleman* and *Arizona State Legislature*.
	- WAIT - but it wasn't the legislature as a whole in *Coleman* either.
## Dissent
- The house is suffering a concrete injury bc it has a vested interest on how its own members get selected.
	- Majority - but the whole point of a democratic legislature is that they don't get to choose how they get selected!
- The majority is too concerned with applying federal separation-of-powers principles to a state government over which they lack jurisdiction.

# State Government Standing
# Massachussets v. EPA
- ***Massachussets v. EPA*** (2007) - State sued over a law that would've allowed more emissions from new vehicles and thus contributed to coastal erosion. Holding: it has standing over this law: state is a coastal landowner, and has a "quasi-sovereign interest" so it could protect on behalf of its residents.
	- PROBLEM - new cars are only one of many factors that emit CO2 and cause coastline erosion.
	- **COURT RELAXED CAUSATION AND REDRESSABILITY FOR STATES:**
		- Causation: you only need partial causation
		- Redressability: you only need to help make the problem less bad
	- A state has "special solicitude" due to its "quasi-sovereign interest" - this gives them a special leg that makes establishing standing easier. Unclear how far that goes.
- Texas v. United States (5th Cir. 2015) - state has standing over law that impacts driver license eligibility bc state issues and subsidizes the driver's licenses. Upheld by SCOTUS without an opinion bc of the 4-4 split.
- Department of Commerce v. New York - federal directive requiring citizenship question on census. states suing had standing bc it could impact reporting rate and thus affect their eligibility for federal prgorams. This is not "speculation" on the basis of third party conduct bc its the "predictable effect of Government action on the decisions of third parties."
# California v. Texas
593 U.S. __ (2021)
## Background
- After *Sebelius*, SCOTUS maintained constitutionality of ACA's individual mandate via the taxing power. Congress then reduced the individual mandate tax to $0. Texas argued the ACA was now unconstitutional bc they came from neither the commerce nor taxing power.
- Texas's argument: ACA costs us a lot of money because of its reporting requirement.
## Holding
- No standing

- Dissent: yes standing through inseverability theory.
	- *Standing through inseverability theory* - if we have standing over any one provision of a law, we have standing over every provision of the law. 
	- Majority doesn't say it's an adequate theory here. It just says that it wasn't properly brought up.
# More Recent Decisions
### Haaland v. Brackeen
599 U.S. __ (2023)
- Texas challenged Indian Child Welfare Act on equal protection wrt child adoption policies. Makes the same reporting and inseverability arguments as *California v. Texas*
- No standing through inseverability **in this case** - maybe in other cases?
- Also, the state does not have equal protection rights - its the parents. They're the ones suffering this injury.
	- Longstanding precedent: state can't sue for injuries suffered by its residents!
### Biden v. Nebraska
600 U.S. (2023)
- SCOTUS case challenging Biden's student loan relief program in 2023.
- Missouri had standing bc it had a state agency (MOHELA) that received processing fees based on the number of accounts it administered. Biden's action would've directly reduced the number of accounts.

- Dissent: MOHELA would've had standing, but it's unclear that Missouri state law gives the state enough control over MOHELA to sue on its behalf.

- No discussion of "special solicitude."
### United States v. Texas
599 U.S. ___ (2023)
- Texas alleged an injury due to lack of enforcement of a federal immigration law (not arresting unauthorized immigrants).
	- Financial injury - they're taking on more social services costs bc of so many undocumented immigrants.
- SCOTUS - there is no cognizable injury here according with history and tradition. In fact, we have precedent that **no party has a judicially cognizable interest in the prosecution or non-prosecution of another.**
	- This precedent comes from *Linda R. S. v. Richard D.*
- Thomas concurrence - no redressability. Statute prevents us from granting any relief that would help plaintiffs.

- Conclusion: courts are no longer treating states as special plaintiffs a la *Massachusetts v. EPA*. Unclear whether it's still good law!
