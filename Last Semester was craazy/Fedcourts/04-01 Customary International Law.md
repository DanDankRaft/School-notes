# Review from Last Class
- Federal common law - legal rules that do not purport to be derived from constitutional or statutory command.
	- This is the one definition that everyone can agree on. If you're a textualist, there is a lot more that seems like common law. If you're a purposivist, a lot more seems like it comes straight from the statute.
- Modern federal common law created after *Erie*:
	- Recognition that US courts don't *discover* common law, but *create it*.
	- *Erie* said there is no "general" common law, but there can still be "specific" federal common law in some circumstances.
- Four enclaves of federal common law:
	- State v. state lawsuits
	- Admiralty law
	- U.S. govt contracts / property
	- Foreign affairs
- Test for when federal common law precludes state law:
	1. Area of uniquely federal interest - defined quite broadly. Easy to demonstrate.
	2. Significant conflict between federal policy and state law (in operation) - very fact-bound, specific.
- Military/govt contractor defense (*Boyle*) - a specific federal common law thing we can apply! Depending on the test above.
	- Common law doctrine stating that contractors are shielded from liability under FTCA.
	- Even if it does apply, there's still a test for asking whether the party is liable.
- One note - the federal policy/interest underlying the common law can suggest some things about what the common law actually is. IE, whether and to what extent it preempts liability.

# Sosa v. Alvarez-Machain
542 U.S. 692 (2004)
- Alvarez was charged in abstentia for torture and murder as part of his involvement w a drug cartel.
- US tried extraditing him, but Mexico said no.
- He was then kidnapped in Mexico, by DEA, and brought to the US.
- In an earlier criminal case, he claimed "you can't abduct me and criminally prosecute me"
	- SCOTUS: That doesn't affect the court's jurisdiction. This doesn't violate extradition treaty, bc the extradition treaty doesn't specifically prohibit abduction...
	- But he wasn't convicted!
- He then sued his abductor for a civil action for arbitrary arrest, under the Alien Tort Statute (ATS)
	- He is suing his abductor (Sosa), and the United States government!
	- ATS is part of the 1789 Judiciary Act, establishes that federal courts have jurisdiction over lawsuits filed by foreign nationals over torts committed in violation of international law.
## Legal Question
- Does the ATS grant Alvarez a cause of action against his abductor?
## Holding (Souter majority opinion)
- No.
- There is no cause of action against the United States government bc of a waiver in the FTCA.

- ATS is a purely jurisdictional statute - it does not create causes of action.
	- If all you have is jurisdiction, courts *usually* cannot create common law!
- When can the courts recognize / create common law here without preexisting causes of action?
	- History: in 1789, if the court was hearing a claim through the ATS, it would not have thought it was *creating* any new claim, they would've merely thought they were *discovering* or *bringing cognizance* to preexisting common law!
	- Three torts that people agreed on: piracy, violating rights of state ambassadors, and violation of safe conduct
		- Violation of safe conduct: if people come through your country to another country, they should be able to go through here safely.
		- Big consideration in 1789: letting British people travel safely through the US!
	- Court here: we're not completely ruling out common law causes of action, but we're very cautious about it! We should exercise a ton of judicial restraint.
- The Test:
	1. This cause of action is Widely accepted & specifically defined
		- Widely accepted in the *international community*, and probably the cause of action itself has some sort of international character..
		- Specifically Defined - at least as much as piracy, violation of safe conduct, rights of ambassadors.
	2. Would allowing this create bad foreign policy consequences?
		- They're trying not to get in the way of the legislative and executive branch in trying to control foreign policy! E.g., signing an international treaty but making it non-executing in the United States!
		- Obvious criticism here: courts are really bad at considering the foreign policy considerations of their actions!
- Applying here:
	- Arbitrary detention - does not fly!
		1. Not widely accepted & specifically defined:
			- Not specifically defined - it's extremely broad!
		2. Bad foreign policy considerations!
			- Court's reading here: would allow anyone to sue about any arbitrary arrest by anyone anywhere in the world.

# Kiobel v. Royal Dutch Petroleum Co.
569 U.S. 108 (2013)
- Nigerian citizens living in the US filed a lawsuit against non-US corporations, for conduct occurring abroad.
- Presumed jurisdiction under ATS.
## Legal Question
- Does ATS apply to extraterritorial conduct?
## Holding
- No. The "presumption against extraterritoriality" applies here too.
- There needs to be some sort of "domestic tie" - like domestic conduct happening in the United States - for the ATS to apply.
- Before this decision, lower courts presumed that it didn't apply bc all of the conduct in *Sosa* applied in Mexico

## Nestle USA, Inc. v. Doe
593 U.S. __ (2021)
- Nestle sued for using child slavery in West Africa.
- Argued that they can overcome presumption against extraterritoriality 
### Holding
- Mere corporate presence or general personal jurisdiction over a corporation is not enough to overcome presumption against extraterritoriality.
- ALSO, restricting ATS to the pre-established categories:
	- Piracy
	- Violation of safe conduct
	- Violation of rights of ambassadors.
