Under the AEDPA, petitioners are barred from bringing a federal Habeas petition with a claim that they failed to exhaust in state court due to procedural errors. (*Wainwright*). He cannot raise his *Brady* claim because he failed to exhaust it in state court. He has two potential ways of overcoming it here:
1. Arguing via the cause and prejudice test (*Wainwright*) that he could not bring up his claim because of the *Brady* violation.
2. Arguing that his new evidence is so compelling that it could render him completely innocent (ie, *House*-style gateway claim of factual innocence)/

## Using Additional Evidence as Gateway Claim
Even if Gates cannot overcome the *Wainwright* test via a traditional *Brady* claim, he can use the additional evidence to bring a gateway claim of factual innocence. ## Standing
Agey may face some challenges in overcoming standing, because he wasn't he one who was imprisoned - Gates was. Agey might have to argue that he has third party standing for constitutional violations faced by Gates.

First, Agey has to demonstrate that has standing on his own merits. That requires demonstrating an injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability (*Lujan*)

Injury-in-fact: Agey's injury is that he is angry - he "was the victim, and that ADA made him feel like a criminal." This makes this part of the analysis challenging, because injuries have to be concrete and particularized (*Lujan*). Even though Agey's injury is not necessarily tangible, it may still be a valid intangible injury (*see TransUnion*). But this depends on how the court characterizes it. A pure injury to conscience can be a concrete injury (*Hippocratic Medicine*), and unlike that case, Agey has an even stronger argument because he was compelled to testify and did not face any real alternative of refusing to do so. In contrast, the defense may choose to characterize this instead as a kind of "ideological opposition" or disagreement with government action (*Schlesinger*). We can easily distinguish such injuries here because the caselaw here is typically about laws passed by the legislature, and not the actions of a rogue DA's office. Finally, the defense can also argue that our injury arises out of an interest in the mere "prosecution or non-proseuction of another," which is widely recognized to not be a valid injury (*United States v. Texas 2023*).

Even if we prove that there was a *concrete* injury, it may not be particularized to us. We were not the only people who were affected by coerced testimony. There could've been other witnesses, including in other trial, and of course many defendants who were convicted because of such false testimony. If the defense here argues that this injury was "wrongful conviction," we were merely members of a class of plaintiffs, but not one of the individual in that class who actually suffered an injury (*see TransUnion*). This is why it's so important for us to establish that our injury was purely the result of whatever *emotional* harm we suffered, such as feeling like a criminal or having to testify against a close friend.

Causation: there is no question that our injury was "fairly traceable" to the allleged due process violations of ADA Fleener and the city of Corinth (*Lujan*).

Redressability: Redressability has to be proven separately for each form of relief sought (*Lyons*). I don't doubt that we can demonstrate redressability for damages. But it may be harder with the injunctive relief. For that, we must show a high likelihood that without that injunctive relief, we would be injured again. Especially after the 70 minutes segment that exposed this conduct, ttestimontieshere's a very low likelihood that the DA's office will continue coercing such testimony with false subpeonas. And there's an even lower likelihood that they'll coerce Agey.

Remaining steps of third-party standing:

The second step of third-party standing is showing a close relationship between us and the plaintiff, such that they rely on us to exercise their constitutional right. We had to testify against them, so this isn't merely a hypothetical right (contrast w. *Kowalski*). But it's not the kind of concrete professional relationship between a doctor and a patient (*Singleton*), or a buyer and a seller (*Barrows v. Jackson*). Ultimately, Gates does not rely on Agey to exercise his due process rights, he relies on his lawyer. Maybe I have sanding?
Our best counter-argument here is that in a criminal trial, a defendant relies on witnesses from both sides to be truthful in order to enjoy due process. And that this lawsuit is about us being deprived of our ability to be truthful.

The final step is that the third party faces some practical obstacle in ajudicating for themselves. Here, Gates will have a really hard time raising this claim in court (*Barrows*). He would face serious procedural barriers to raising due process claims via Habeas because of the AEDPA's exhaustion requirements and procedural defaults. And he would not be able to bring any 1983 challenge unless his conviction is somehow removed. The defense's only argument is that these do not entirely bar Gates from raising these claims, because depending on how is pending Habeas petition goes, he might still be able to raise it (*Kowalski*)

## 1983 Claim Against ADA Fleener
1983 allows us to bring a 1983 claim for damages against a state official in their personal capacity for operating under the color of state law while violating someone's constitutional Due Process rights. But we would have to overcome Fleener's official immunity.

### Absolute Immunity
Prosecutors face absolute immunity for functions intimately associated with the judicial phase of a criminal trial (*Imbler*), but they do not face such immunity for investigative activities (*Imbler*, *Burns*, *hypnosis case*). Our strategy here would be to argue that Fleener violated our due process rights via an investigative activity.

In *Burns v. Reed*, a prosecutor was found to have engaged in an investigative activity for using illegitimate methods to extract a confession from a defendant. That sounds like what we've faced here. But in the same case, they were found to have absolute immunity for misleading the court by admitting this evidence without disclosing his method of receiving the confession.

The ADA will not receive absolute immunity for claims that he violated our due process rights when coercing our testimony. This is an *investigative* function. He may try to distinguish our case from *Burns* by arguing that what he extracted was not a confession, or that he only used illegitimate means to invite us to talk, are irrelevant here. These are still tasks done for investigative purpose - they are not intimately associated with a trial. Finally, he may argue that supervising a trial (*Goldstein*) is a prosecutorial function receiving absolute immunity. But he did not *supervise* this trial - that would've been his boss DA Summers. And again, we are merely arguing that events happening *before* the trial are ineligible for absolute immunity.

### Qualified Immunity
An official receives qualified immunity when there's a violation of a federal constitutional right, and this violation was *clearly established* at the time of the incident (*Harlow*).

Violation of a constitutional right: ADA Fleener violated due process by using using threats to fabricate evidence (Sixth Circuit Case). Plainly, his conduct was "so outrageous, that it may fairly be said to shock the contemporary conscience." (Seventh Circuit Case) He sent a fake subpoena to coerce a witness to produce false testimony. While some caselaw from other areas on this course asks courts to give greater deference to lawyers due to their extensive outside training (*Connick v. Thompson*), this additional training makes Fleener's conduct much worse - he should've known better.

Clearly established at the time of the incident: this is more difficult, because it requires us to find a case on point (*Brosseau*). This makes the Sixth circuit case less useful here, because it only talks about "threats by police." ADA Fleener is not a cop - he's a prosecutor. It's difficult to tell whether the Seventh Circuit case is useful here, because we are not told anything about it except for its holding. The closest case we have is *Burns*, but even there, the confession involved hypnosis and the main suspect, not a legal threat and a witness.

Our best argument is that this conduct is so outrageous and obviously unconstitutional that it does not require us to find a case on point (*Hope, Taylor v. Riojas*). Here, the fact that we used the seventh circuit case which includes similar language in order to overcome the first prong may help us.
