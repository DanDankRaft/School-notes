# Review from Last Class
- Implied Private Right of Action - federal statute doesn't say anything about whether a private party can sue to enforce.
- Even before Cort/Cannon, the court interpreted a private right of action into securities regulation!
- *Bivens* was also built on the private right of action framework!
- Cort/Cannon framework was designed to limit PRoA
- Four step balancing test:
	- Private right established? same as *Gonzaga* test.
	- Legislative intent - what did the individual legislators actually think (subjective test)
	- Legislative purpose - what is the overall purpose of the bill, based on reading its text (objective test)
	- Is this an area of law traditionally reserved to the states?

- Another major precedent from *Alexander v. Sandoval* - a regulation cannot create a private right to sue (by itself!)
- New test (*Sandoval*):
	- Private right established? same as *Gonzaga*
	- Is there any alternative remedy meant to be used when the defendant breaks the law? including cutting federal funding? If yes, presumption that it precludes any alternative remedy.

- Over time, *Sandoval* framework is gradually gaining more traction in the circuits, but Cort/Cannon test is still commonly used.

- What is the difference between the *Gonzaga* test and *Sandoval* test?
	- Step 1 are *exactly* the same in Gonzaga, Sandoval, and Cort/Cannon! Precedents regarding this step are interchangeable!
	- Step 2:
		- Under §1983, the presumption is that it creates a remedy unless there's an alternative *private* remedy.
		- Under *Sandoval*, the presumption is that if there is any alternative remedy, including a non-private remedy, there is NO implied private right of action.
- One consequence of this:
	- If the law only establishes a public remedy: NO *Sandoval*, YES §1983
	- If the law establishes *no* enforcement mechanism:
		- And you're suing a state: NO *Sandoval*, YES §1983
		- And you're suing the federal government or a private party: YES *Sandoval*, NO §1983
- One reading: the goal of *Sandoval* is to make it impossible for courts to read in a private right of action going forward!

# Federal Common Law
- Shouldn't there be no federal common law? Bc of *Erie*?
- There is no federal general common law. There is specific federal common law.
	- Can't make common law about contracts generally, but you can make laws about very specific types of contracts
- Before Erie - presumption that courts *found* common law. After Erie - understanding that courts *make* common law. That made courts squeamish.

- Definition of federal common law: a legal rule that does not purport to be derived from statutory or constitutional command.
	- This is not about debates on statutory interpretation!

### Where does federal common law apply?
1. State v. state cases! SCOTUS original jurisdiction!
	- Why not use state law? Bc no state wants the other state's law to apply!
2. Admiralty law.
	- Constitution is pretty clear that no state's laws apply here
3. Rights and obligations of the United States - such as federal contracts, duties/obligations of federal offices.
	- Uniformity is really important for contracts with the federal government!
4. Foreign affairs
	- We don't want states to intervene in foreign relations! We want **federal common law to preempt state law in these situations!!** "dormant foreign affairs clause"

### Federal common law we've encountered in this course
- Third party standing - a prudential standing doctrine! Does not purport to come from either the constitution or federal statute.
- Abstention: Younger, Pullman, Colorado-River.
	- Rooker-FEldman is NOT common law. It purports to be based on the court's appellate jurisdiction, either from Article III or a Statute.
- Sovereign Immunity? Including arguments about *Bivens*!
	- Yes common law: Based off of common law from England
	- Not common law: What about Amendment XI?
	- Underscores that there are edge cases where it's not clear whether sovereign immunity 
	- Bivens specifically: there's an argument that it's derived from the constitution, because if the constitution establishes some sort of right it expects that there'll be an enforcement mechanism for it.
- Official Immunity?
	- Not common law: it's an interpretation of §1983..
	- Yes common law: it also protects federal officials! And even when they do, they say that §1983 was written with common law in mind!
- Mootness? or is it derived from Article III
- Political Question Doctrine? or is it derived from Article III
- Nationwide injunctions? Before *Trump v. Casa* - lots of rules about it without any constitutional or statutory authority for it.
	- After *Trump v. Casa* - comes from Judiciary Act of 1789..

# Boyle v. United Technologies Corp.
487 U.S. 500 (1988)

- Boyle was a marine helicopter pilot, killed when his helicopter crashed, but he was unable to escape from his helicopter.
- His dad sued the manufacturer of the helicopter based on Virginia product liability law. Design defect.
- Appeals court overturned this, arguing that this went against "federal common law"
## Legal Question
- Is there such a thing as federal common law, and does it apply here?
- If there's a conflict between federal common law and state law, when do we apply federal common law? IE, when does it preempt state law?
## Holding
- Yes. Defendant wins.
- Federal contractors asked congress multiple times for a provision in the Federal Tort Claims Act that would shield them from liability. Congress said no!
	- So the only authority saying that contractors are shielded from liability is a new federal common law this case creates - a defense for military contractors!

> [!Info] Test for applying federal common law:
> - There is a "uniquely federal interest" implicated here, and
> 	- Grove: This is typically pretty easy!
> 	- NOTE: when the dispute is purely between private parties, and federal govt. is not affected by the outcome, it's not a uniquely federal interest anymore. Chain of causation is too speculative!
> - There is a "significant conflict" between the federal policy or interest and the application of state law

- Applying here:
	- "uniquely federal interests"
		- federal contracts are a uniquely federal interest
		- immunity for federal officials for actions take in their course of duty.
			- Not really used anymore, bc nowadays there's the Westfall Act - which says that when a federal official is sued for common law claims, the US is substituted as the defendant.
	- Significant conflict between federal policy and operative state law
		- Direct conflict between the government requiring a design to certain specifications and product liability doctrines.
			- IE, Direct conflict between the terms of the contract (create helicopter according to specifications set by the federal government) and state product liability law (create helicopter according to terms that an injured plaintiff claims would be necessary)
		- Conflict between required design and policy of reducing costs for US govt!
		- FTCA discretionary government exception. Deciding how to design a helicopter is a discretionary function of the engineers involved!
