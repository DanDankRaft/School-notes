# Congress's Power to Create Non-Article III Courts
- The text of the constitution only mentions one type of federal court - Article III courts where judges have salary and tenure protections.
	- Article III literalism: this means that Congress can only create Article III courts!
- *And yet*, other types of courts exist!

- Worry: Congress can create so many non-Article III courts that Article III courts stop mattering. Nowadays there are way more non-Article III judges than Article III judges.

# Northern Pipeline Construction Co. v. Marathon Pipeline Co.
- Congress cannot create new bankruptcy courts without Article III protections, because these courts are too powerful.

- Congress considered making these new bankruptcy judges full Article III judges. But **federal judges** lobbied against it! It would "diminish the prestige of their position"

- There are categories of non-Article III judges that are allowed:
	- **Military Court martials.** The judicial branch needs to be very deferential to military matters, so it makes more sense to put these courts under congressional control.
	- **Territorial courts.** Congress has plenary power over territories, so we're deferential to Congress. ALSO, if the territory is about to become a state, we wanna be able to fire all these judges (or turn them into state judges)
	- **Adjudication of public rights** Government-conferred "benefits:"
		- Immigration
		- Patents
		- Tariffs
		- Social Security
		- Veterans Administration (VA)
		- Administration of public lands
		- Revenues (customs and taxes)
		- Licenses (e.g., licenses from FCC)

- Why does it make sense to let non-Article III courts adjudicate public rights?
	- These are deemed "privileges." The government giveth and the government taketh away.
	- **Sovereign immunity**. These are all adjudicated against *the government.* If the government has the right to not be sued at all, it also has the right to get sued on its own terms (incl. under non-Article III courts)

- **Public rights is a very broad term.** You can easily redefine it and change what's allowed.

- J. White's dissent: I can't believe we're saying the essential Article III power is just "non state law claims."
- J. Brennan: Article III literalist. We have to grandfather in these categories, bc they've been around since 1789. But everything else should be forbidden.

# Thomas v. Union Carbide Agricultural Products Co.
- Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act
- This statute established mandatory, binding arbitration over data sharing between two parties (a private right)
- **Holding:** it's ok for a non-Article III courts to handle binding arbitration, even when it's over a private right! As long as those private rights are enmeshed in the same statutory scheme!

# Commodity Futures Trading Commission v. Schor
- It's ok for an agency tribunal even for private law counter-claims to be handled by an agency tribunal
- Rationale: consent. As soon as you agreed to go with an Article III you waived your right to only handle these in Article III courts.
	- This is such a segment that it's not gonna erode Article III
	- **Note:** here, agency tribunal was an *alternative* to Article III litigation!

# Granfinanciera, S.A. v. Nordberg
- **Article III court required when you should have a right to a jury - like common law claims and common law - like claims.**

- Fraudulent Conveyance Action - when you fraudulently take funds out of a bankruptcy estate to prevent it from being sold off in the bankruptcy action. This is a common law claim!

- 7th amendment gives you a right to a jury. If you don't have to litigate under an Article III court, you don't get a jury! That matters!

- Brennan + Scalia: best buddies in protecting the power of Article III courts!

# SEC v. Jarkesy
- SEC filed an action against Jarkesy for defrauding a bunch of investors.
- His proceeding was handled by an administrative court.
- **Legal Q:** does the 7th amendment prevent this issue from being handled in an administrative court.
- **Holding:** invalid proceeding. Proceeding must've been held in Article III court!
- When there's a statutory claim that's "really analogous to a common law claim," you retain 7th amendment right!
	- Common law claim: fraud!

- Why would the SEC prefer to handle this in an administrative proceeding?
	- They prefer litigating on their own turf!
	- They didn't want a jury trial! They thought they might lose!
	- Securities litigation is expensive and time-consuming. Jury trials make that worse!

- Another problem: SEC was looking for civil monetary penalties. That feels like damages! That fits better under an Article III court!


- What happens to the administrative state if every time that an agency tries enforcing a common-law like statutory claim, and request common-law like damages?
	- They'll just do way less enforcement! Bc it makes enforcement so much more expensive!
