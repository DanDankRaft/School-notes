# Political Question Doctrine
- Different from standing, and ripeness, and mootness.
	- It's not about *who can sue*
	- It's about whether **the issue** goes beyond Article III judicial power.
- Typically, its about constitutional issues that the other branches should resolve between themselves.
- There's scholarly debate about whether its merely a matter of prudence or about Article III. But SCOTUS says it's Article III

## The Test:
> [!info] Court cannot resolve a question where:
> 1. There is a textual commitment in the constitution to resolve the issue in a political department; OR
> 2. There is lack of a judicially discoverable and manageable standard for resolving it: IE, we cannot come up with a test.

# Zivotofsky v. Clinton
566 U.S. 189 (2012)
## Background
- Plaintiff was the mother of an American citizen born in Jerusalem. She asked that her son's passport put his place of birth as "Jersualem, Israel."
- Problem:
	- State Department has policy stating to not put a country as part of someone's place of birth if they were born in Jerusalem
	- Congress passed Section 214(d), which gives citizens born in Jerusalem the right to have their place of birth include "Israel".

- Lower courts: can't decide on this, bc "political question doctrine" says judiciary cannot decide which country Jerusalem is in.

#### Diversion: establishing standing
- Injury-in-fact: *deprivation of a statutory right*, the violation of which creates standing.
	- Does this survive *Spokeo* and *TransUnion* which mention that deprivation of statutory right requires being grounded in an
	- Is this a conscience injury? Is it a psychic harm injury?
- Causation: the defendant - the state department - deprived the party of this statutory right.
- Redressability: the court can issue 
## Legal Q
- Does the "political question doctrine" prevent the court from ruling on this issue.
## Holding
- NO. Courts can rule on this issue.
- The question before them is not "Can court determine which country Jerusalem is in"
- It is "whether 214(d) is constitutional" - which is a very clear and typical responsibility of the courts.
	- FWIW, the court decided that the statute is invalid bc it's up to the president to decide who controls Jerusalem

> [!info] Political Question Doctrine is largely about how the court interprets the given issue

## Concurrences and Dissents
- Concurrence: merely using the kinds of "textual, structural, and historical evidence of the kinds that courts routinely consider" is not a good argument that this isn't a political question. (Majority made this argument)

# Goldwater v. Carter
444 U.S. 996 (1979)
> [!failure] This is a political question because:
> 2. There is no textual committment to neither branch, meaning the court cannot adequately come up with a test
- Pres. Carter signed mutual defense treaty with Taiwan
- Congress sued, arguing the president didn't have the authority to sign a treaty with a right of termination, but with no explanation for how that right should be executed.
- The issue: who has a right to abrogate an international treaty
	- NOTE: constitution doesn't give an answer to this question!
- Holding: **Political question, case dismissed**
> [!info] Because there's no textual commitment to neither the courts nor the political branches, the court **cannot come up with a standard**

- This dispute is between two political branches - who have the resources to settle this themselves. This is not a private litigant v. the state..
- Courts don't wanna be involved in foreign relations! Specifically, if the treaty is not abrogated, that would've created immense 

- Technically a plurality decision, but treated as a majority opinion ever since.

#### Diversion: legislative standing
- DC circuit held that there was *institutional standing* here - treaties are made with the president and two thirds of a Senate. If two thirds of the Senate don't like it, you're injuring the institution of the Senate.
	- At the time, the DC circuit was considered very lenient with granting legislative standing.

# Rucho v. Common Cause
588 U.S. 684 (2019)

> [!failure] This is a political question bc:
> 2.  it's too messy to resolve whether a partisan gerrymander is fair. Questions of how much is "fair enough" or "significant enough" etc; but

> [!success] We cannot say that this is a textual commitment bc:
> 3. gerrymandering is not exclusively up to congress, we already set precedents about this.
## Background
- Plaintiffs sued state governments for excessive partisan gerrymandering.
- Lower courts ruled for plaintiffs.
## Legal Q
- Are claims of partisan gerrymandering "justiciable" before a federal court?
## Majority
- **No - this is a political question.**
- The Elections Clause of the constitution allows state legislatures to regulate elections, *subject to the laws of Congress*. So why couldn't they just say that there's no textual commitment to the courts?
	- Because of their opinions on racial gerrymandering
- Any standard for resolving a gerrymandering? political? question must be grounded in a "limited and precise rationale" and be "clear, manageable, and politically neutral."
- The question here is about what standard should be used if a certain electoral map is "fair." There is no answer to this question that is "clear, manageable, and politically neutral."
	- Generally, questions about what's "fair" are political questions.
- Beyond the political question test: in a deeply partisan era, there's no map the court could draw that would be accepted as legitimate.
## Dissent
- This is NOT a political question
1. The question here is whether a 14th amendment right as been violated - that's not political!
2. The court's contention that there is no good standard for this is false - lower courts have already converged on one (the *extreme outlier approach*)

- Response from the majority:
	- Who's to say whether the extreme outlier approach is an objective matter of fairness?
	- There are other ways of creating fair maps - like creating an independent redistricting commission.
		- Irony: in the past, Roberts argued that such commissions are unconstitutional.
	- Congress could step in.
	- You could go up to state courts - but some state courts argued that this is a political question under their doctrines too.
