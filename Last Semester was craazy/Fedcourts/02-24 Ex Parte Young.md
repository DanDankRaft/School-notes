# Review from Last Class
- State sovereign immunity in state courts exists, but it doesn't come from the 11th amendment, it's rooted in historical tradition older than the constitution itself.
- A state can still waive its immunity if it wants to.
- Can Congress convince states to give up its immunity?
	- *College Savings Bank* - a state doesn't waive its immunity by just engaging in some activity (constructive waiver is a bad theory)
- But, Congress can encourage a state to give up its sovereign immunity via the Spending Power
	- *South Dakota v. Dole*, *Sebelius* - general test for whether this is a constitutional use of the Spending Power to compel state action.
		- General welfare
		- Unambiguous
		- Nexus between conditions and purpose of the expenditure
		- No violation of any other constitutional guarantee
		- Cannot be coercive
	- Addition to this - the unambiguity requirement is much stronger in a sovereign immunity case! The waiver language must be "express and unequivocal"

# Ex Parte Young
209 US 123 (1908)
- Minnesota enacted a law fixing the price of train tickets.
	- Violating this law (selling a ticket above the fixed rate) could incur heavy fines and a prison sentence of up to 5 years.
	- Problem: it would be really hard to convince any ticket seller to break the law for the railroad company and go to court!
- The whole point: Minnesota tried creating a law that nobody would ever break, nobody would ever challenge, and nobody would ever challenge in court.
- Shareholders in the railroads challenge the rates as a violation of the 14th amendment. They win in federal court and gain a preliminary injunction to prevent AG from enforcing this law.
	- There is no *Younger* abstention bc this was filed before there was any state criminal proceeding on this matter.
- Young tried enforcing the law via state court anyways. He was found guilty of acting in contempt of court and put in prison. Petitioned SCOTUS for writ of habeas corpus.
	- He thought he was protected by the 11th amendment bc he was acting as an agent of the state!
## Legal Question
- How the hell do you sue a state for doing something unconstitutional?
## Holding
- Sovereign immunity applies to both monetary and injunctive relief (*Seminole Tribe*)
- And typically, you cannot sue an individual (like Young) for violating the constitution, bc the constitution only restricts state action!
- And typically, you wouldn't just want an injunction against an individual AG, you want an injunction against all AGs, in present or future.
	- But if you're suing the office of the attorney general, aren't you acting as an agent of the state?
- One option: the Supremacy Clause means that the constitution overrides all unconstitutional case laws.
	- Therefore, a state official carrying out the duties of an unconstitutional law is not acting as an agent of the state - because the state cannot enforce a law that is unconstitutional!
	- Problem: but how can you violate the constitution if you're no longer an agent of the state?
> [!info] What the court says: AG Young is a state official for the purposes of the 14th amendment, but he is stripped of the sovereign immunity to which states are entitled for the purposes of the 11th amendment, because he is enforcing an unconstitutional law.
> This is largely regarded as a "legal fiction" but its ok bc its a good fiction


> [!important] You can sue a state official, in their official capacity, for injunctive or declaratory relief, if they carry out an unconstitutional task or perform a statutory violation on behalf of the state.

- The court doesn't have to decide the merits of the case before deciding whether an Ex Parte Young exception applies, but they do matter!

### Historical lens
- At the time, this reaction was shocking! And progressives hated it!! Bc state governments tended to be woke and progressive and SCOTUS was evil and republikkkan (*Lochner*)
	- The justice who wrote this majority opinion also wrote *Lochner*!
- This is one of the most important examples of a law of unintended consequences in the long term...

# Whole Woman's Health v. Jackson (2021)
- Texas SB 8 allows anyone but a government official to sue anyone else for aiding or abetting abortion. Bounty enforcement.
	- $10k per person involved in each procedure + attorneys fees (only for plaintiff)
	- No state official was actually enforcing the law!
- Plaintiffs brought a pre-enforcement challenge, suing a bunch of state officials, trying to bring an *Ex Parte Young* action.
	- This was pre-Dobbs, so it seemed clear to everyone that it was plainly unconstitutional
- Similarity to *Ex Parte Young* - create a law so draconian that nobody would want to violate it and challenge it in court!
## Legal Question
- In order to render the law unconstitutional, who do you sue?
### Holding
- **You must sue state officials who are enforcing the law**. Which officials those are is up to state law and state courts.
- SCOTX said that none of the officials sued have no enforcement capacity under this lawsuit, so none of these named defendants could be sued to bring forward this injunction.

- The question before the court was whether the court was going to acknowledge how the state was using loopholes to avoid liability and bend the rules to close that loophole.
- Takeaway: you can simply get around Ex Parte Young by setting up a bounty system

# Prospective v. Retrospective Relief
# Edelman v. Jordan
415 US 651 (1974)
- Plaintiff sued state officials in Illinois for violating federal disability benefits law (Aid to the Aged, Blind, or Disabled Act (AABD))
- Won lawsuit, and demanded
	- Injunction to prevent future violation (prospective)
	- Injunction to repay us all the benefits you retroactively denied us (retroactive)
- NOTE: state didn't bring up state sovereign immunity claim until appeal. This is OK!
	- While waivable, you can bring it up at any step in the process!
## Legal Question
- Can you get retroactive relief?
## Holding
- No.
- **You can only get prospective relief, not retroactive relief.**
- **Ex Parte Young is always about future relief**
- Even if an order has **an ancillary effect on the state treasury**, it's fine as long as its prospective. It doesn't matter how big the monetary impact is, it just has to be indirect.
> [!info] This is not a clear-cut difference. In most cases, you can argue either way. Even the court acknowledges this!
# Milliken v. Bradley (II)
433 US 267 (1977)
- *Ex Parte Young* case about school desegregation. Lower court ordered costly state programs such as remedial reading programs.
- Legal Q: is this allowed?
- Holding: Yes! It's part of a plan that looks forward about fixing it and ameliorating effects. This is a prospective plan with ancillary effect on the state treasury.
	- **This is a plan that operates *prospectively* rather than *retrospectively***
