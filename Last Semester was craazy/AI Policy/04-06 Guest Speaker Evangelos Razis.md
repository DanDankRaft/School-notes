## Committee Assignments and Jurisdiction
- Senate - individual member offices can develop specific policy expertise, so committees don't matter as much
- House - committees are more important bc the committee staff are the main locus of *personal expertise.* Only people with enough expertise to draft bills from scratch, have subject-matter expertise on technocratic issues!

- Types of hearing necessary to get a bill out of the commerce committee:
	- Educative Hearing - on subject matter and issues
	- Legislative hearing - on early draft
	- Sub-committee markup - including drafting amendments
	- Full committee markup - including drafting amendments
- One result of this process : higher quality legislation, more bipartisan legislation.

- It's hard to get a bill through a committee that you do not sit on!
- Which committee cares about a subject matter changes over time.
	- Early on, Research committee (deals with science stuff) was the main committee dealing with AI. Bc it was seen as a mostly academic issue.
	- Nowadays, it's the commerce committee bc it's seen as a big commercial regulation issue!

- What makes AI interstate commerce? The entire tech stack crosses state lines! The training data comes from across state lines! The hardware supply chain is mostly international!

- Is there a concern that Congress will swallow up state abilities to legislate on a tech issue due to preemption? In theory, yes. In practice, Congress regulates tech so rarely that it's not an issue. In fact, there's an issue of regulatory gaps!
	- Some Congressmen are extremely averse to express preemption.
	- Other Congressmen want preemption on some issues, but not on others.

- How is institutional knowledge maintained in a committee? Especially as committee membership and leadership changes?
	- Very hard! Turnover is high regardless of changes in house membership!
	- Not as bad for dedicated committee staff (these are very coveted jobs), but still occasionally happens when majority changes.

- On bipartisanship:
	- There are some issues where members are more comfortable with bipartisanship.
		- Ex: Screen Act - federal version of the *Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton* law. It's very hard for anyone to vote against it, so they try to draft it in bipartisan ways.
	- There are some times when staff intentionally try to draft a bill to be more bipartisan.
		- Ex: when a Republican drafts a new bill to regulate FTC, they might intentionally include provisions about equity that Democrats want.
