# Soft law approaches
- what mechanisms do we have beyond statutory law?
- On Monday - guest lecture from Christoph Winter who is one of the authors of the EU AI act.


- What's the definition of soft law?
	- It's non-binding. Why does that matter?
		- Ex: FDA's non-binding bulletin that all companies follow. Allows the FDA to quickly revise and withdraw regulations without having to go through the traditional regulatory process.
		- Agencies have venues to suggest how they think about certain areas of the law without entrenching them as hard law.
			- Actors have an incentive to follow these suggestions because it's otherwise a bad look
- Examples of soft law:
	- FDA has a non-binding bulletin that all companies follow.
	- Industry standards
		- Can gain traction if customers start expecting and demanding those standards - like LEED certified.
	- Social norms

- What makes people follow soft law? What makes it a soft law *legitimate*:
	- Social norms
	- Industry adoption
	- Money - insurance companies may require you to follow the soft law.
	- Procedural pedigree - announcing a standard or white paper backed by other stakeholders in the space gives it more legitimacy.
	- Can you enforce it?
		- It's complicated!
		- If courts enforce it as an industry standard for contract or tort law, it's enforced! You make yourself legally liable if you don't follow it!
		- But at this point - the line is blurry. Is it still soft law if courts recognize and enforce it?
- The extent to which its followed depends on the degree of legitimacy that actors have.
	- This is significant in AI because we don't have social norms in this space yet and we don't have organizations with entrenched legitimacy.
	- Part of what Anthropic is trying to do with the Claude Constitution is establish an industry standard or social norm and give itself legitimacy off of that.

- Soft law vs hard law is a spectrum.
	- There are soft laws that are enforced by courts:
		- Industry custom
	- There are hard laws that nobody really follows:
		- Speed limits
		- Underage drinking
		- Media piracy

- Question: does soft law require a figurehead organization to create it?
	- Not necessarily.
	- But one good instance of this is a Self Regulatory Organization (SRO) like the National Association of Realtors.
		- But they also have flaws! Like regulatory capture.

## Undersea cables example:
- send 99% of data across continents
- A lot of similarities between AI and undersea cables:
	- Strong economic reliance - 99% of trans-oceanic data goes through privately owned undersea cables.
	- Highly susceptible to attacks and misuse.
- What has hard law done well in undersea cable domain? What has soft law done well in undersea cable domain?
	- FCC gets to control where undersea cables are laid. It asks: what kinds of traffic is going in that location? What consequences to the environment would be if you lay it there?
	- One consequence of this: it's hard to lay new undersea cables, and it's impossible to do it quickly.
	- Laying undersea cables is expensive. We are reliant on private actors to build and maintain our undersea cable network. We don't have a public alternative.
		- **This private sector reliance pushes us to rely on soft law in order to sustain the industry!**
		- We want to maintain an incentive for private actors to build more infrastructure!
