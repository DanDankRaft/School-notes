- If a bill gets passed in the last 10 days of the legislative session, the legislature doesn't have enough time to override the veto. *The Post Adjournment Veto*
	- For this reason, the governor prefers to delay the passage of major bills such as the budget until the last 10 days of the session.

- The veto is a legislative power given to the governor.

- The power to call a special session *and* the power to issue a post-adjournment veto make the governor more and more powerful the closer the session is to closing.

- The office of the governor works with legislators to "fix" legislation to their liking in order to avoid vetoing them
- Legislators feel very personal about vetoes, so Governors avoid them as much as possible.
- Pre-passage, the office of the governor assigns a policy analyst to the bill. the analyst's job is to track the bill through the entire process.
	- The policy analyst assigns each bill a score from 1 (best) to 5 (worst), adjusting the score as it gets amended.
	- If a bill's score goes down over time, the office will intervene to try to improve it.
	- If a bill's score is low and it looks like it's about to pass, the office will intervene.

- Once a bill passes, there's a bill review meeting between the:
	- General Counsel
	- Policy Director
	- Legislative Director
	- Chief of Staff
- The four of them each vote on whether to: sign, veto, or file without signing. They create a recommendation for the governor.

- Signing and filing without signature do the same thing. Governors don't really care about the difference.

- Let's say that an unconstitutional bill passes the legislature and comes before the governor's desk, would you recommend that he vetoes it?
	- One perspective: it's not the governor's job to strike down unconstitutional bills.
	- Another perspective: we all have a duty to enforce the constitution!!

- Some interesting exercises of governor's power:
	- Sign a bill, with a statement saying its only constitutional in very narrow circumstances. De facto defeating the purpose of the bill without vetoing it.
	- Vetoing a bill that you don't actually oppose, saying "it's more important to do X. Once you pass X, I will sign this bill."
		- Abbott did this in 2023 with property tax relief. He then called a special session that included property tax relief but not this bill.

- If your party controls the legislature, you should be looking for reasons to veto rather than to sign. Start from the presumption that you're going to sign it.

# Constitutional Authority of the Veto
- *Ex Parte Perry*: the governor's power to exercise a veto may not be circumscribed by the Legislature, by the courts, or by district attorneys.
	- Specifically, you can't make it a crime to threaten to veto
- Former Governor of Illinois is now in prison for bribery: he was accepting bribes to veto legislation. 
- *In re Chris Turner*: SCOTX holds line item veto of branch appropriations constitutional - courts have uniformly recognized that it is not their role to resolve disputes between the executive and legislature.

- On average, about 13% of filed bills pass.

- The legislature cannot, during a special session, override a veto over a bill passed in a preceding regular session.

- What's the legislature's recourse if a bill is vetoed?
	- Override it; or
	- Pass a new bill that does the same thing


# Line-Item Vetoes
- The governor can line-item veto 'items of appropriation'
- The legislature sought to veto it by combining as many things as possible into single items.
- *Jessen v. Bullock* - in order for a line-item veto to be constitutional, it has to be an "item of appropriation":
	1. An indivisible sum of money
	2. dedicated to a stated purpose.
- What was Jessen missing? an actual amount of money!


- Why does the governor veto line-items in the first place? He usually doesn't due it out of a noble need to balance the budget.
	- Things he considers "wasteful spending"
	- Expand his executive authority!!
- After the veto, the comptroller requests an advisory opinion from AG's office. Why?
	- To dissuade people from suing him!
- AG argues that vetoes are constitutional.
- LBB and legislature publish their own opinions about this, strongly disagreeing with the governor and AG.
- Lt. Gov is backing the governor. Why? bc he wants to be governor one day.

- What recourse does LBB have? not much. They can sue but they'd probably lose.
- If this went the LBB's way, what recourse does the governor have? Use his appointment power to prevent the money from actually being spent.

- A lot of those vetoes were to things that nobody really cares about 


- Our line-item veto is actually weaker than many states - gov. cannot reduce an item of appropriations.
- But he can exercise his "rescission" power...

# Vetoing Resolution
- Gov can approve or disapprove all resolutions, except those relating to adjournment
	- Simple resolutions - congratulatory items, only passed in one house
	- concurrent resolutions - waive sovereign immunity, or request congressional action.
	- Joint resolutions - constitutional amendments (require 2/3 of both chambers)
- Unclear whether joint resolutions are subject to a veto.
	- Abbott is making subtle moves to hint that he'll try doing that.
	- But, it'd be pretty redundant anyways since they already require two thirds anyways.

# Takeaways
1. governors rarely actually veto bills. They work with legislatures
2. Signing or vetoing is a pass-fail test. It relies on a lot of political and personal factors.
3. The controversy over the 2015 line-item vetoes is not about budget savings, they're about strengthening the power of the governor.
