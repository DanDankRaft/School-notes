# State of the State Address
- Governor must give one at the beginning of each session.
	- Must recommend such measures he deems "expedient"
	- Traditionally given via joint session of House and Senate
	- Trying to turn political objectives into policy proposals - that's a difficult job.
	- Includes governor's emergency items and budget proposal
		- Budget proposal is not constitutionally required.
## Emergency Matters
- Typically, legislature cannot consider legislation in the first 60 days. It's also the only time you can file bills, unless you suspend the rule with a 4/5th vote.
	- First 30 days reserved for bill filing
	- Second 30 days for committees.
- Bills pertaining to the governor's emergency items can be voted on in the first 60 days!!
- Because the House rarely holds floor votes on anything before mid-March, Emergency designation has far greater effect in the Senate.
- Declaring something as an emergency item makes it far more likely to pass.
- The governor files the emergency items with the secretary of state.
- The items themselves tend to be kind of vague.
- How partisan / bipartisan they are tends to be influenced **by the results of the previous year's election**

- Common power play: if a legislator blocks passage of an emergency item, the governor threatens to call a special session.

- The governor is by statute the chief budget officer. He has to present his proposed budget on or before the state of the state address
	- The governor's proposed budget tends to be missing numbers and budget tables. It's more of a Concepts of a Plan

- What's the budget required to contain:
	- list of appropriation for the current year
	- expenditures for each of the two full years preceding
	- Amounts requested by various agencies for each of the following two years.

- You may notice that the proposed budget does not comply with any of the statutory requirements. This is bc the point of the proposed budget is exclusively a political tool.
- The actual starting proposal is prepared by the Legislative Budget Board.

## Bully Pulpit
- Governor gives state of the state address, submits a budget to the legislature, issues emergency proclamations.
- None of these actions have a lot of binding influence, they're merely an extension of the bully pulpit - he must use political capital to actually achieve his means.

- Examples:
	- In 2017, after legislature passed a budget and governor signed it, he announced a hiring freeze on all governor agencies. Why? bc the governor wanted to close a budget shortfall. But no member of the legislative or executive branch challenged the memorandum.
	- Why? bc the governor appoints the members of all the boards that lead all of these agencies. The leadership of these agencies and institutions isn't going to challenge the guy that appointed them!

- Governor has no authority to issue binding executive orders or memos in most instances.

- Appointments are the most important power of the governor! They're what give the governor's demands actual teeth!


- Should a Republican governor oppose a Republican incumbent in a primary?
	- Historically, it wasn't done. But in 2018 Abbott did that. He **actively recruited** their primary opponents!
	- Why? Bc the legislators supported a moderate house speaker that opposed some of Abbott's legislative agenda.
	- In the short term, 2/3 of Abbott's candidates lost. But in the long run, it was a deterrent effect on those incumbents, pushing them to support more of Abbott's agenda!
- In 2024, Abbott doubled down. Specifically in order to kill Republican opposition to school vouchers. He coordinated with a national pro-voucher PAC, started a statewide school voucher campaign.
	- Minor problem - if you're a voter in a rural district, and you hear that your rep voted against school vouchers, you'd support him even more.
	- So instead, the ads lied about targeted incumbents.
	- Out of 21 Republicans who were primaried - Abbott won 11, and 7 other incumbents retired.

# Executive Orders
## State Board of Plumbing Examiners  - Sunset
- **Sunset Advisory Commission** - Every state agency has a sunset provision in its organic statute, saying that after some years, they have to be reviewed to see if they've met their goals. If they haven't, the agency is abolished. If they have, the commission members pass a bill extending their life.
	- The sunset commission is made up of legislators.

- In 2019, the state board of plumbing examiners was sunset. This would've taken away certification from all plumbers in Texas and eliminated occupational standards.
- Governor issued an Executive Order ignoring the statute and saying that the state board of plumbing examiners would stay in business.
- Technically, he didn't have any authority to do that. He's directly going against words in a statute.

- Why didn't the legislature object?
	- At least in practice, they were fine delegating that power to Abbott.

# Special Sessions
- Called in 60 of the 89 legislatures.
- George W Bush was the only recent governor who didn't call one.
- The governor is less powerful when the legislature is in session.
- Typically: only the governor can call a special session. UNLESS, the session is about impeaching the governor. Then, the house and senate can call themselves.
- The governor also controls the *agenda* of the special session. If a bill is not on the "call" of the special session, it can be killed via a point of order.
	- Governor can add things to the call of the session at any moment.
- Special sessions are limited in duration to 30 days, but governor can call as many special sessions as they want.

- The governor's power to threaten a special session makes him more powerful the closer a legislative session is towards ending.

- In practice, the legislature has a lot of prerogatives during the special session:
	- Doesn't require the governor to define the details of his calls with precision - but just in general ways. The legislature has the authority to define the specifics of how they'll answer that call.
		- Case: defendant loses in trial court, but wins on appeal bc the court was improperly created in a special session where the call didn't authorized the creation of a court.
	- Doesn't require the proclamation of the character 

- *Baldwin v. State* - D was guilty of failing to pay an occupation tax created during a special session. The call stated that one of the purposes of the session was to "reduce taxes." Court found that the proclamation embraced the whole subject of taxation, and thus authorized any law to do with taxes.


- Any special session will include some bills filed outside the stated purpose of the call of the special session. Two approaches to that:
	- One approach - grant at least first reading to every bill and figure out if its in the call later.
	- Another approach - the chair reviews every bill for germainess before deciding whether to grant it a first reading.

- Generally, if a bill in a special session is outside the call and isn't vetoed, it will become law.
	- *Maldonado v. State*
- HOWEVER, if a bill is still going through the legislature, and is subject to a point of order before it passes, it *should* be sustained.

### Enrolled bill doctrine
- When the legislature passes a bill, the legislature cures them of all procedural defects - constitutional or otherwise. So you can't strike down a bill in court for that.
	- *Jackson v. Walker* - this includes bills passed outside call of special session. And you cannot go back to legislative record to find evidence in order to invalidate the law on procedural grounds.
- THE ONE EXCEPTION:
	- *ATPE v. Kirby* - legislature passed the bill, heads up to governor for signing. BUT before governor signs it, someone strikes a number with a sharpie and replaces it with something else.
	- SCOTX - "when the bill passed by the legislature is different from the one signed by the governor"


- During a special session, the governor's real power is not really about defining a special session call, but about vetoing the bill.
	- One way to think about it: the veto power is the governor's remedy for bills outside his call.

- During a special session, the presiding officer of the house or senate can just kill a bill by declaring that it's unconstitutional.
