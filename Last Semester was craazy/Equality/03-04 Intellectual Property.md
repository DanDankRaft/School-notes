- distributive justice is in every part of the law! Even in private law!
- Critical Legal Studies - 80s movement, did distributive analysis of private law.
	- Law & Economics - responded by saying "nuh-uh. You can't do distributive justice via private law"


- Every part of the law constitutes the structure of society. And distribution is the product of every part of society.
	- There is no such thing as a "natural distribution" of resources, so we have to pick some sort of structure.
	- There is no reason why this shouldn't happen in IP.
- Why the pushback?
	- Private law is built on individual freedom and economic efficiency, it's not really about distributive justice. It's a mismatch.
	- Therefore, you're likely to get bad results by re-shaping private law around distributive justice.
	- It's also a paternalistic approach - especially in contract law.
	- Therefore, distributive justice should happen through taxation and transfers.
- It's a 19th century view of "private vs public spheres.."

# Intro to Intellectual Property
- Bad layman's idea: Property is a relation between a person and a thing - *ownership*
- Correct idea: Property is a relationship between people with regards to resources.
	- *with regards to resources* is what distinguishes it from torts and contracts.
- Intellectual Property is property law where the resource is *intangible* - ideas, information, etc?
	- Patent - the resource is *practical technological knowledge*.
	- Copyright - works of authorship, expression.
- Each type of IP has unique features that justify unique approaches to how we treat them in the law!

# Why have intangible property rights?
- Informational goods are *public goods*
- Public goods:
	- Non-rivalry - it's the kind of resource that one person can use without hindering the ability of others to use it. Most tangible resources don't have this!
	- Non-excludability - it's really hard to prevent others from using this good (wrt IP, once it's shared or published)
- It's much more costly to create new types of information than to copy / re-produce it.

- The Enablement Problem: it's very hard for people who create new intangible resources to profit off of their work, or even break even.
- The Incentivization Problem: it's very hard to convince people to create new intangible resources.
## How does IP solve these problems?
- Right to Exclude - solves both problems by letting the creators of intangible resources earn money.

> [!info] There is no first order interest wrt intellectual goods to get a right to exclude others
- The creator of the intangible resource does not have an inherent interest in excluding others. They only have an interest in fair or sufficient compensation.
- There is no first order interest to exclude others wrt any non-rival good. Only rival goods lead to this interest.

## There is no such thing as a free lunch
- Every time we let people enjoy intellectual property for free, others have to pay more for it.
	- Ex: if some people get free access, others have to pay more.
	- Ex: if the government funds the creation of some public domain IP through a grant, the government has to pay a ton!
- This is bad bc a higher cost is a barrier to entry.

### Dead Weight Loss
- It seems like there's a struggle between consumers and authors - consumers want weaker IP rights, while authors want stronger IP laws. 
- But stronger IP laws reduce creativity - it means there are less existing materials you can iterate on!
- And weaker IP laws reduce the incentive to create new works in the future!


> [!info] The Big Questions:
> 1.  Do we need any laws to solve enablement and fair compensation problems?
> 2.  Are IP laws the best mechanism to solve enablement and fair compensation?
> 3.  If we go with IP laws, how do we balance the good and the bad?

# Alternatives to IP
- Government Prizes - if you create something, the government will compensate you with money. The money comes from the taxpayers.
	- Subsidies / Grant - like a prize, but you get it before you create something. We already do this with a ton of scientific research!
- Private patronage - this requires no IP litigation. It was also the most common intangible resource mechanism before the rise of modern IP laws!
- Commons - you release the works into the commons, with no expectation of fair compensation.
	- OSS - also an example of *peer production*
- Government creating intangible goods themselves.

# IP and distributive justice?
- IP is about creates an *unequal distribution* wrt who has access to certain resources
	- Most obvious example: IP limits who can manufacture and profit off of a drug. It means that certain people will not be able to afford that drug!
	- Even in copyright: think about educational materials. Access to culture and democratic discourse, and who gets to participate in it. Even as entertainment, it creates an unequal distribution wrt what people want to enjoy.
- Under IP, what's distributed is:
	- access to intangible goods
	- cost of production
	- surplus profit from the production of goods
	- because intangible goods are derivatives, it also distributes *who has the ability to create new stuff*


- Traditional trademark law is not about fostering a personal brand, it's about preventing consumer confusion. Modern trademark law *is* about fostering one's brand and the value of a brand.

# Kapczynski Piece
- Externalism - let's always look at alternatives to IP. Whenever there's an issue with the distribution of intangible resources, let's look beyond just modifications to existing IP issues
	- ALSO: because IP creates a price barrier, it relies on markets. It has unique distributional problems because it has the same problems that markets have!
- The three downsides of IP as a market-based mechanism:
	- Distributive justice
	- Privacy problems
	- Efficiency

### Upsides of Markets?
- Pricing is a discovery function - it enables us to discover what kinds of goods are in the highest need/demand, and creates an incentive for creating them.
	- Alternatives, like government production or prizes, are centralized in the hands of a few people. This means that they can completely miss the mark wrt what people want or need.
### Downsides of Markets
- Markets are influenced only by those who have the ability to pay. This means that those without an ability to pay have less access to intangible goods.
	- There's a catch-22 here. The naive solution to this is to weaken IP protections. But this reduces the amount of intangible goods available in the first place.
	- Bracha - this problem is not insurmountable. There are ways of increasing access without reducing the incentive to create
		- ex: grant exceptions to medical patents in poorer markets. It hits a good balance - massively increasing access in the regions where it's most needed, while barely decreasing profits.
		- ex: US copyright law includes an exception when adapting a work to make it accessible for the blind.
- The Political Economy of Intellectual Property - it's a mechanism that tends to destroy itself. 
	- Relying on markets as a signal puts pressure on authors to maximize profits for its own sake. How do you make more money as an author? by getting more IP rights. It incentivizes authors to lobby government to give themselves more and more IP rights until it becomes impossible to create new works.


## Neglected and Rare Diseases Problem
- **Neglected and rare diseases problem** (a production problem) - just bc you equalized access, doesn't mean you've created a market incentive to produce highly needed works.
	- Why is that a problem? If we weighed that there is more net utility in spending resources on a minor drug that helps the many than an important drug that helps few..
		- Incommunsability: we cannot trade one's health for others' happiness. These are not things we can compare on the same scale!
	- There's a mismatch here between the market signal and the right social criterion. And this criterion cannot merely be solved by strengthening or weakening
