- What are the legal ramifications of male-centric design choices?
	- Consumer Expectations Test: minority test. is it dangerous beyond the extent which would be contemplated by the ordinary consumer testing it
	- Risk-Utility Test: basically a negligence test. But some jurisdictions also require the plaintiff to present a "reasonable alternative design." In those jurisdictions, there is a cost-benefit analysis done between the current design and the alternative.
		- Ordinary user - the costs and benefits are measured wrt the hypothetical average person expected to use the product "in a normal and foreseeable manner"

- The argument: when a product is marketed to a general population (not just men or women), we analyze the utility of this product in the aggregate. This aggregate analysis masks the fact that a product may have vastly different utility for men v. women.

- The solution: subgroup analysis. Factor in the utility for women and the utility for men separately from just the aggregate utility.
	- We already do this: utility for children, utility for people with allergies.
	- Problem: in those instances, courts analyze this as failure to warn. That doesn't work so well for *women*.

- Imagine that we judge the utility of a product as a threshhold question - it must at least have $u_{min}$ 
	- Traditional approach: only the aggregate utility is judged against u_min
	- Wasserman's proposal: u_min must be judged against both the utility for men and the utility for women.

- Limited Subgroup Considerations - women's safety should not fall below the minimum threshhold.
- Robust Subgroup Considerations - women's safety should be equal to men's safety.



- Additional wrinkles:
	- Causation - you need to prove that the original design is the but-for cause of your harm. But these kinds of claims are hard to prove for a particular plaintiff.
		- You need to introduce some sort of *probabilistic causation*, relying on population-level data.
		- Alternative: shift the burden to the defendant. Make them prove that they were not the but-for cause in this particular case. Burden-shifting already happens in situations where you have informational asymmetry.

	- Reasonable Alternative Design - already hard for plaintiffs to demonstrate, especially with complicated products. Here, it's harder bc there's already less data available for what makes products safer for women!
		- Solutions: utilize industry leaders (e.g. standards organizations)
		- Recognize that some products are not actually complex
		- Dispense with this requirement - makes sense here bc the whole argument is that there is a systemic factor that prevents such designs from existing.
