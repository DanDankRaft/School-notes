# Social effects of AI
- Starting point: AI models are controlled by big corporations! Facebook, Google, OpenAI, etc. All of the social implications start from here:
	- Ex: that's why all of the questions about productions are so focused on "AI taking our jobs!!"

- There are also questions about the training process too! These involve a lot of human participation that we typically see as invisible. This leads rise to all kinds of questions about equity here!



- The industrial revolution was a very gradual thing.
- What is qualtitatively different about AI compared to other technological innovations?
	- It's much more generalizable than other technological innovations. It is very easy to see how many different fields it will impact!
	- Opacity - there is so much work going behind the scenes to make AI work, and most people know absolutely nothing about it.

- Alienation:
	- Under modern capitalism, you get a pair of shoes by going to the store and buying them. It's a commodity.
	- That hides the complex social system of who made the sneakers and why and the power dynamics and all of that.
	- In a pre-commodity system, you're very exposed to the production of the shoes. You might've even made them yourself!
	- Commodification also reduces everything to a single scale (market value) and makes everything exchangeable. 

- One idea: what AI does is commodify types of goods that could never be commodified before. Knowledge and art!
	- And one thing that's scary about that.. is that it means that the people who control those models now have complete power over what kinds of art and knowledge are produced.

- Hockey stick of innovation: something about the industrial revolution massively accelerated the rate of technological innovation. Why is that? What happened?
	- Bracha's opinion: braapitalism. Once you have a profit incentive, you have a new and powerful incentive to innovate, to find uses for your innovations, and for spreading your innovations. 
	- Law and Political Economy: an emerging? school of legal thought that thinks we should think of technological and economic developments as being shaped by our legal institutions.

- One of Bankler's arguments: capitalist societies tend to have internal dynamics that push us towards maximizing profits.
	- Red Queen dynamic: if you're a producer, you have to innovate as quickly as possible because otherwise somebody else will innovate before you and you'll lose!
	  "If you wanna stay in place you have to run as fast as you can"
	- The red queen dynamic has advantages! it leads to tons of technological innovations! And at least some of them had tremendous positive 
	- Downside: you don't have to be socially productive in order to maximize profits. You can come up with innovations that exploit your employees, or sabotage your competition.

- Capitalist innovation creates *technological dynamism*

- Bracha doesn't buy into complete market determinism. He does think that scientific innovation did not play a role in AI emerging in 2022. But he does think that market forces pushed the general direction of where scientific innovation is going and where it will go in the future.

- Legal constructivism: school of thought that all of societal relations come downstream from law.
	- AI supply chain paper thinks everything comes downstream from politics - power struggles within society.

# Bankler and social class
- Marxist conception: bourgeoise and proletariat. About the relationship to the means of production.
- Bankler proposes a 3D-compass of class:
	- One axis is propertied vs non-propertied (do you have to work in order to sustain your basic needs). Similar to Marxist class but not exactly the same.
	- Another axis is manual vs cognitive labor ()
	- Third dimension is legal status as a full human person (serfdom, slavery, etc, only relevant to historical analysis)
		- This is where race, gender, etc fit into the analysis of equality!
- Bankler is also concerned with the PMC (Professional Managerial Class)
- PMC - Professional Managerial Class
# Bankler paper - where can we go with AI? 
- Our current trajectory - acceleration of continuing automation and *deindustrialization*, dislocation of knowledge from employees to systems, disenfranchising them.
- Another trajectory - more similar to German and Japan (corporate oligarchy?)
- A third trajectory - Nordic model. 
	- Strong social networks mean that people are less dependent on the market!
	- Very strong unions mean that employees' social power is *independent* of the market value of their skills!
	- Technology seems to have produced something different from the US and UK. More equal distribution of the fruits of technological advancement, but also of the relationship between labor and production.
- Fourth model - AGI eliminating the need for labor entirely.
