# Review of Functions of Distribution
- Dworkin - equality of resources but with an auction. We take care of people disadvantaged by brute luck, but not of people disadvantaged by option luck.
- Rawls - the difference principle (maximin). The difference principle is actually a type of prioritist view!
	- Leximin - a version of maximin, but it says that after we've done all that we can for the worst off group, we prefer doing the most for the second-worst group, then the third-worst group, etc.
	- Rawls is critical of leximin bc he thinks its not practical. If we have capacity to help the second-worst group, we usually still have capacity to help the worst-off group.
- All welfare maximization criterions are also functions of distribution. They're a kind of priority principle. Give priority to those who gain the greatest benefit!

- Parfit and Frankfurt both say - our discussion is about resolving a social justice problem. We ask what's the most right way to distribute goods in society as its own goal - not as a way of serving some other societal goal.
	- This matters because we often think of equality as valuable as a means to pursue some other goal. 
	- Ex: political equality - equal voting rights, equal political spending.

# What's wrong with (telic) equality anyways?
- **The Leveling Down Objection**
	- It taps into a deeper objection - telic equality suggests that there's something good with sameness, in and of itself. The point of leveling down examples is to demonstrate that there are instances where we achieve equality, but nobody argues that it's actually a good outcome.
- **Bottomless pit problem:** There are some people where getting them on an equal footing with everyone else costs so many resources that it would require exclusively investing in them, at the expense of anyone else.
- **Equality is comparative** - this is an argument that Parfit and Frankfurt raise. Bracha thinks that it's not a good argument.

- So why do we intuitively feel as if equality is worthwhile for its own sake? We are making an unjustified jump from equal concern (we care equally about each human life) and equal outcome. One does not necessarily imply the other!!

# Frankfurt Reading & Sufficiency
- We figure out a standard for minimum sufficiency (ie, a floor) and then do whatever we can to make sure as many people as possible meet that standard.
	- Once someone gets to their sufficiency level, we don't care about what happens next.
- A sufficiency approach tends to go hand-in-hand with equality of capabilities.
- Problems with sufficiency:
	- It doesn't solve the bottomless pit problem. If the threshold is in terms of capabilities rather than resources, you might have some people that become a bottomless pit of resources!
	- The way to solve that is to set the bar very low. But if the bar is very low, then this doesn't achieve much equality.
	- If you set the bar very high, you might not have sufficient resources to meet the threshold, and you're more likely to run into a bottomless pit principle.

- There are some cases where, even with infinite resources, it's impossible for some people to hit the threshold.
	- E.g.: we set a threshold for 5th grade reading level. But some students will never be able to meet that threshold, even if we throw infinite resources at them.
## Objective or Subjective Threshold?
- Do we think that everyone should have the same threshold? Or do we think that each person's threshold depends on their wants? needs? The amount of resources in society?

- If we divert resources from the people who are way above the threshold, **we can do so much more**. Thresholds always create an opportunity cost.
	- And the same with bottomless pits. If we divert resources from people where meeting any threshold is futile, we can achieve so much more for the average person.
