# IP policy and theory
- Example: Swiffer - a cleaning product created by P&G

- Why do we have IP laws?
	- Incentivizes creating new things!
		- IP is a public good: non-excludible and non-rivalrous.
		- Not a good explanation for trademarks!
	- Trademarks: incentivizes the creator to maintain a good brand reputation!
		- Incentivizes creating information..
- What features of a Swiffer might be protected by IP law?
	- Trademark: the name, the logo, the color scheme?
	- Patent: the mop, portions of the mop (utility patents), the design (design patents)
	- Trade secret: cleaning solution formula
	- Copyright: instruction manual, the mop as an art exhibit?
- Costs to P&G
	- Direct legal costs
		- Acquiring patent, registering copyright, etc
		- Enforcement costs: suing infringers!
	- For trade secrets: keeping the information secret carries a lot of indirect costs!
	- PR - you don't wanna seem like a bad guy when trying to enforce your IP!
	- R&D: You actually have to create something! Can't just copy other people..
		- Even the engineers talking to the patent agents to create a patent instead of doing R&D...
- Costs to the public
	- Consumers
		- Less competition! Higher prices.
	- Competitors
		- Licensing, designing around
		- Potential competitors might just not enter the market
		- Discourages innovation
# IP law and its limits
- IP vaccine case study
	- patent!!!
		- the vaccine itself -> covers all uses
		- method of making the vaccine - not possible bc someone else patented it -> covers less than everything
		- method of using vaccine -> covers all uses
	- Nonenforcement pledge covers the vaccine itself, but not the method by which it is made.
		- We have to ask the client - how did they figure out how to make this?? are we running into trade secret problems??? Client turnover??
	- probably cannot develop a patent over the vaccine itself
	- But they *can* develop a patent over the manufacturing process!!
	- Patenting this could be really bad PR..
	- Valuable creation: the vaccine!!!
	- Trademark our cool and awesome vaccine name! Like Ozempic

# Oullette and Masur
- Trade secrets - governed by the Uniform Trade Secrets Act in 49/50 states.
- Design patents - have very different requirements from Utility Patents (the kind i'm used to) and Plant patents.
	- Disclosure only requires drawings
	- "Ornamentality" requirement - the design cannot be dictated by its function.
	- Infringement - an ordinary observer familiar with the prior art would consider the two designs to be "substantially the same"
- Copyright - governed by 17 USC + some limited state law protections for works not protected by federal copyright.
	- Copyrightability requires a *modicum of creativity* and *independent creation.* You can copyright a work that is substantially similar to some prior art if you weren't aware of it.
	- Cannot copyright:
		- Useful articles - only aesthetic features conceptually separated from its useful articles
		- Ideas themselves - only expressions of ideas
		- Facts
	- Copyright protects from reproduction, distribution, performance, display, and adaptation into derivative works.
- Trademarks - governed by the Lanham Act 15 U.S.C. § 1051
	- Since trademarks are not meant to incentivize production of intellectual goods, but to protect the identity of brands, the laws and policy arguments around it are very different from the rest of IP.

- All World Trade Organization members are bound by the 1996 Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS)
	- Members are required to uphold the intellectual property rights of all other members.
