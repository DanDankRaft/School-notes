# Intro to Trade Secrets
- Trade Secret law was largely state-level until DTSA. But very similar to UTSA.
- Before UTSA, trade secret law evolved out of the common law of torts.
- Elements of Trade Secret claim:
	1. **Information eligible to be a trade secret**
		- Economically valuable
		- Not generally known
	2. **Reasonable efforts to maintain secrecy**
	3. **Misappropriation** - D acquired secret information wrongfully.
- Evidence used to prove/disprove elements #1 and #2 often overlap
	- The statutory definition of "trade secret" incorporates both elements

# Statutory Definitions
## UTSA § 1(4)
> (4) "Trade secret" - information, including a formula, pattern, compilation, program, device, method, technique, or process, that:
	(i) derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known to, and not being readily ascertainable by proper means by, other persons who can obtain economic value from its disclosure or use; and
	(ii) is the subject of efforts that are reasonable under the circumstances to maintain its secrecy.

## 18 U.S.C. § 1839(3) - Defend Trade Secrets Act
> (3) "Trade Secret" - all forms and types of financial, business, scientific, technical, economic, or engineering information, including patterns, plans, compilations, program devices, formulas, designs, prototypes, methods, techniques, processes, procedures, programs, or codes, whether tangible or intangible, and whether or how stored, compiled, or memorialized physically, electronically, graphically, photographically, or in writing if—
> 	(A) the owner thereof has taken reasonable measures to keep such information secret; and
> 	(B) the information derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known to, and not being readily ascertainable through proper means by, another person who can obtain economic value from the disclosure or use of the information
# Mallet and Company Inc. v. Lacayo - Subject Matter
## Background
- Mallet manufactures release agents - a common baking ingredient.
- Lacayo worked for Mallet for over 20 years before leaving for a competitor.
> [!info] Common origin of trade secret claim
> Employee leaves for competitor
- Mallet presented a general list of types of information, an "overall body of knowledge" (formulas, manufacturing processes, sales and cost data, R&D knowledge, customer feedback, business information of Mallet's customers), that it classifies as its trade secrets.
- It also sees Lacayo's "general know-how" from working for Mallet for 20+ years as a trade secret.
- Some of the alleged trade secrets include information disclosed in Mallet's publicly released patents.
- No dispute that the information was acquired wrongfully.
## Procedural Posture
- Mallet received preliminary injunction (FRCP 65) to prevent disclosure of trade secrets from lower court. This injunction is what got appealed.
	- This also matters outside of preliminary injunction issues bc of *Twombly* requirements in actual pleading.
## Legal Q
- Is this information eligible to be a trade secret?
- Does this pleading meet the specificity requirements for a preliminary injunction under FRCP 65?
- Can a trade secret include publicly disclosed information?
## Holding
- Not specific enough to satisfy FRCP 65.
	- The subject matter of a trade secret must be described with enough specificity to distinguish it from general knowledge and knowledge that comes from merely being skilled/experienced in the trade.
		- Policy: enable D to understand the boundaries of trade secrets
	- P bears the burden of defining the trade secrets with sufficient specificity

- A trade secret cannot be publicly disclosed information, but can *include* publicly disclosed information. Ex: a confidential compilation of public information
- An employee's "general know-how" is not a trade secret, but specific facts the employee knows can be trade secrets.

- Does a customer list count as a trade secret?
	- Probably no. It's general knowledge. You can ask people in the know or look at trade publications to figure that out.
- Why didn't they seek patents over the formulas?
	- *Obviousness* - the formulas are relatively simple. They have very few ingredients and are easy to make.
	- This is a major difference between patents and trade secrets!!
	- A trade secret does not have to be novel. It may have occurred to somebody else or even used by another before the owner discovered it. It just cannot be **generally known or readily ascertainable to competitors**

# In-class problems
## II-3 - Cancer Cure
- Researcher left Research Co. who was trying to develop a cancer cure. Moves to another company seeking the same cancer cure. Uses his prior experience of unproductive avenues of research to speed up development and help his new employer come up with a cure before Research Co.
- Is this trade-secret eligible?
	- Knowing what not to do is tremendously valuable, so it *can* be a trade secret
	- But without further information, it's not specific enough. What if it's general knowledge?
- Policy concerns:
	- We want to encourage a company to invest a lot of money in R&D and their employees. Letting them leave for a competitor and take all their trade secrets with them defeats this purpose!
	- But... if we take that to the extreme we prevent employees from switching jobs!
	- Do we want the Researcher to just stand there and let his new employer try all these different things that don't work?
## II-4 - Church of Scientology
- Church of True Belief founded around closely-guarded secret scriptures. A breakaway sect forms the new House of Absolute Truth. They take a copy of the secret scriptures.
	- The Church of True Belief guards knowledge of the scriptures behind donations
	- The House of Absolute Truth uses the scriptures to attract followers
- Are the scriptures economically valuable?
	- YES - the materials help the church solicit donations!
	- No - policy arguments.
		- How are we encouraging innovation by keeping this scripture secret? This is a church, not a business.
- Who can hold trade secrets?
	- Older restatements - **only businesses**!
	- Modern sources - business or **other enterprise**
- Holding: Church of Scientology lost
	- Although scriptures are economically valuable, the church didn't pursue this argument bc then it would've lost its tax exemption as a church!
> [!info] Example of how IP practice is about the client's business strategy as much as it is about the law itself

# UTSA § 1 - Definitions
> (1) "Improper means" - theft, bribery, misrepresentation, breach or inducement of breach of a duty to maintain secrecy, or espionage
> (2) "Misappropriation":
> 	(i) A person knowingly acquiring a trade secret, through improper means
> 	(ii) disclosure of a trade secret to another person w/o express or implied consent basically without consent or from a person who acquired it via improper means
(3) "Person" - natural person, corporation, business trust, etc...

> [!important] DTSA is modeled on the UTSA
> But DTSA definition of Trade Secret is slightly narrower than the UTSA
# Casebook 41-72
### History
- Trade Secrets may have existed as early as the Roman Empire
- Medieval guilds and apprenticeship programs acted as trade secrets: knowledge of a craft was intentionally limited to members of the guild
- Modern trade secrets and NDAs emerged during 19th c. industrialization: factory owners wanted protection for more incremental know-how gained on the factory floor, for which patents are ill-suited.
- *Kewanee Oil v. Bicron* (1974): seminal SCOTUS case. Affirmed that federal patent protections do not preempt state-level trade secret law. 
- Trade secrets went through a "soft reset" when the UTSA replaced common law corpus in the 1960s
- 2016 Defend Trade Secrets Act: codified the UTSA into federal law.
## Theoretical Justifications
- Trade Secrets as Intellectual Property
	- *Ruckelshaus v. Monsanto Co.*: SCOTUS considers govt disclosure of a trade secret as a violation of the Takings Clause!!!
	- Like other forms of IP, it is a government policy designed to encourage innovation and market efficiency
		- *Kewanee Oil v. Bicron*: Trade Secrets encourage innovation in areas where patents don't.
	- Unlike tangible property, "property" rights to trade secrets are not exclusive
- Trade Secrets as Torts
	- improper disclosure of a trade secret is a breach of trust and a breach of the duty of "commercial morality" *See* *Eastman Co. v. Reichenbach*
	- Trade secret law creates a commercial value for information:
		- "Arrow's Information Paradox" - people won't pay money for ideas unless they know what those ideas are, but without nondisclosure agreements, disclosing an idea to a potential buyer destroys its value.
## Basic Elements
A trade secret claim:
1. Concerns **subject matter** eligible for trade secret protection. Mostly about creating economic value to the owner.
2. Holder took **reasonable precautions under the circumstances** to keep it secret
3. D **acquired the trade secret wrongfully**
- Most trade secret cases arise when someone breached an express or implied duty to not disclose the trade secret.
- DTSA: exception for whistleblowers disclosing suspected illegal activity. 18 U.S.C. § 1833(b)

## Notes
- Due to specificity problems, courts increasingly require plaintiffs to identify and list all trade secrets at issue early in litigation
- Trade secrets don't require *absolute secrecy*. Disclosure is permitted if its necessary to enable a holder to profit form his secret:
	- Disclosing a secret to employees
	- Disclosing a secret to others pledged to secrecy.
- Concepts that are too well known cannot be trade secrets
- If there is *any* published source disclosing the secret, it doesn't matter how D acquired the knowledge. Even if they misappropriated it from P, they're still in the clear.

# Rockwell Graphic System v. DEV Industries - Effort to Maintain Secrecy
## Background
- Rockwell subcontracts manufactory of parts to third party "vendors"
	- To enable them to manufacture the parts, they give them "piece part drawings"
- How Rockwell protects those drawings:
	- With engineers: keeps drawings in a vault, monitors engineers who use that vault and makes them sign NDAs.
	- With vendors, makes them sign confidentiality agreements including requirement that they return the drawings once they're done, but doesn't actually enforce that. Instead, it considers a vendor's "ethical standrds" before giving it a drawing
- DEV poached a couple of engineers from Rockwell who were fired for stealing drawings.
- They ended up with 100 such drawings.
## Legal Q
- Did Rockwell sufficiently protect its trade secret drawings?
## Holding
- Yes - SJ against Rockwell overturned.
	- None of this is definitive, but a reasonable jury *could* find that Rockwell maintained its trade secret
- Its sharing of the drawings with vendors does not forfeit protection. The vendors are bound by a duty of confidentiality.
> [!note] There can be an explicit or *implied* duty of confidentiality
> BUT, if you know that some knowledge you're about to share is valuable, and you don't ask for a confidentiality agreement, that can be evidence that you didn't take reasonable measures to protect your secrecy!!
- Material question of fact for the third element (wrongful acquisition): is the probability that DEV acquired the drawings through proper means low?
> [!info] "Reasonable" precaution standard - balancing costs and benefits of the precaution.

- *Electro-Craft Corp v. Controlled Motion, Inc.* - mere intent to keep something secret does not meet this requirement. Doesn't make up for security measures that are too lax!
	- Electro-Craft used extremely lax physical security - no locks, signs, badges. Drawings not shredded. No confidentiality policy. No restricted access.
	- Kind of contradicts Rockwell.
- First circuit: "An affirmative step to protect secrecy, not merely ordinary discretion, is required"
# Problem II-6
- Bartender develops a simple formula for an alcoholic beverage. Takes a lot of extreme precautions to keep it secret. A competitor copied his formula and markets it under his name. Unanimous testimony that someone who has experience in the industry can reverse-engineer the drink by smelling and tasting it.
- Did he take reasonable precautions to keep it secret?
	- He would've had to make his customers sign NDAs before they have a drink. But that's not reasonable! This is a bar!
- But if it's so easy to reverse-engineer, it's "readily ascertainable by proper means" which makes it not a trade secret!!
