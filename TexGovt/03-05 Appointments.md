- Senatorial courtesy - a sitting senator is allowed to approve any constituent nominated for appointment before that constituent's name is formally submitted for a vote
	- If even the person from their own area doesn't support them, then the rest of the Senate probably won't.
	- It's a kind of unofficial recognition of the Senate's right to advise and consent (Art 4 § 12(b)).
	- What if the governor wants to appoint a hardliner from your district, but you ideologically disagree with the governor and the appointee?
		- If the governor disagrees with you, chances are that they'd appoint someone you dislike no matter what. It's better to have someone you disagree with from your own district than someone you disagree with from another district.

# Confirmation Process
- The governor can make an interim appointments while the legislature is not in session. In the next regular session, if the Senate does not confirm them, they have to step down.
- This can be abused - if the governor wants someone on a committee that he thinks the Senate won't approve, you make them a recess appointment right after the regular session ends, and then you'll at least have 1.5 years.
- A lot of recess appointments tend to not get confirmed when there's a change of governor.
### Special session
- Until 1999, recess appointments used to expire if there was a special session and the nominees weren't confirmed.
- NOW, it doesn't matter. Policy focus - let the legislature focus on the session call items.
	- Before this amendment passed, special sessions used to drag on forever bc they had to confirm all interim appointments.
	- but, if the legislature does hold a vote on confirmation and reject you, then you have to step down.

- *Walker v. Baker* - Senate may not call itself into session to act on nominations of the Governor.
- Why does the state constitution require legislative confirmation of governor appointments?
	- it was enacted by people who hated big government, and thus wanted more checks and balances.

# Holdover Provision
- Before Nov. 2017: "All officers within this State shall continue to perform the duties of their offices until their successors shall be duly qualified."
- Now: holdover only lasts until the end of the first regular session following the expiration of the appointee's term.

# Legislative Ineligibility
- Article 3 §18 - no member of the legislature may be appointed to any office, during the term to which she's elected, if the pay was increased or if the position is subject to Senate confirmation.
	- Didn't want legislators to create a cushy, well-paying position for themselves and then getting appointed to it.
- Article 3 § 19 - no one holding office would be eligible to run for the legislature during the term to which they were appointed or elected.
- *Wentworth v. Meyer* (1992) - service of the official controls rules under Art. 3 §19, not their term of office.
	- Court says ruling otherwise is at odds with the rule that requires us to strictly construe election law restrictions against ineligibility.

- Article 16 § 40 prohibits any person from holding more than one public office.

# Bully Pulpit
- Governor can use his bully pulpit to pressure appointees to resign. This gives the governor a lot of power to get appointees to do whatever he wants.
	- Gov. cannot actually remove appointments without 2/3rds of the Senate. This has never been used.

- There's a tension between qualification+expertise and bias. If you're qualified in a certain field, chances are you're connected in that field and have personal stakes there.
