- ECJ
	- Anybody who contests EU law (as opposed to national laws) cannot go to a national tribunal. You have to go to a court of justice.
	- ECJ doesn't just handle appeals; it also hears trials!
	- Very often there are companies saying - my country does something I disagree with so I go to ECJ to sue them for violating EU law!
- Two types of EU law:
	- Directives - EU provides high level objectives, each country translates those high-level objectives into national law. Very high risk of misunderstanding and lack of uniformity. But it some fields (like Education) it makes a lot more sense to provide member states the *flexibility*
	- Regulations - specific legislation that applies uniformally. Like the GDPR.

- There's a different version of GDPR that only applies to law enforcement authorities.


- Processor: the one processing the data.
- Controller: the one controlling the data. Decide who gets to access the data. They have the right to control. Sometimes the roles are combined. They are the ones who own the servers!
	- Decides why data is processed. If I give my data to eBay, and they store this data in an agreement with a processor, they're clearly the data controller.
	- Sometimes you have shared controllers.
	- Company collecting data for sale.
	- Processes personal data **on behalf of** the controller.

- There was no thinking about AI back when the GDPR was proposed.
- There is no contradiction between GDPR and AI Act.
	- The two are **fully aligned.**
	- You have to be extremely careful when processing *special categories of data.*
	- Whatever's forbidden in the GDPR is also forbidden in AI Act.
- Processing of very sensitive data is authorized only if it's to fight discrimination or protect fundamental rights.

- Fundamental Rights
	- Defined by EU treaties.
	- Right to travel and live anywhere in EU + a few others.
	- PRIVACY IS A FUNDAMENTAL RIGHT
	- SECURITY IS A FUNDAMENTAL RIGHT - states must protect their citizens.
- Those fundamental rights are NOT absolute.
	- Ex: Freedom of expression. It's a fundamental right, but it is limited in Europe.
	- Ex: Freedom of movement. If you break the law, you can get arrested.
	- Ex: Right to Privacy. If the police needs to investigate someone 
- Fundamental rights can only be broken if there are *grounds and evidence* that they did something bad.

- Because of this fundamental rights difference, there are some differences between the GDPR and state laws:
	- Consent. In the EU, you must give companies *consent* to use their data. In the US, it's opt-out. You must tell a company to not use your data.

- Legitimate Interest - if you sign up to a dating app, there's a legitimate interest from the dating app to transfer your data to some services in order to offer you matches. They're not going to ask you every time for consent to use your data.
	- Big point of fuzziness in GDPR

- EU Digital Omnibus - proposal from commission, sent in November.
	- **Wants to change the definition of personal data**
		- In current definition - any data that can potentially identify someone. So pseudo-anonymous data currently counts as personal data. There's worry that researchers think that pseudo-anonymization techniques are unreliable and can be defeated by a malicious attacker - except for encryption!!
		- The new definition - if the *person who receives the data* is unable to identify you and can no longer acquire additional data that identifies you, it's no longer personal data.
	- **Considering it's a legitimate interest to use personal data for training in order to provide a service to that person.**
		- Lingering q: what does it mean by providing a service "to that person?" Does it mean that specific individual or someone else?

- These are huge wins for companies, but raise alarms for users.
