# AI Safety Institutes As A Vector For Global Governance
- Why do we need AI Institutes specifically? Why not rely on existing institutions?
	- There's a realization that they need a way and skillset to regulate AI.
	- A need to move away from vague discussions about morality and ethics to some sort of concrete policy.
- International governance is very convoluted and confusing - especially rn, it's unclear 

- Nerds talking to nerds is a very different style of conversation from policymakers to policymakers.
	- When they talk about AI, they focus on the mechanics and pieces. **That's important - it leads to decisions that are grounded enough in the real landscape of AI to be enforceable**

## Variation in International AISIs
- UK was first - wanted to make sure they have safety mechanisms and regulations in place before AI starts to actually harm people.
- US came later but had a much higher budget (under Biden). Falls under NIST. Focuses on measurements, standards, testing frameworks. Let people borrow our work.
	- **Investing in AISIs early means having influence worldwide.**
- Japan - a working group made up of a billion different government agencies and *private corporations*. Japan views this as a business issue, and their job is to serve businesses. Their focus is on security in the use and application of preexisting models, rather than the creation and training of models.
	- US and Japan are the most ideologically aligned in their AI approach.
- EU AI office is not an AISI - it's a regulatory body with actual enforcement power.

- Canada - focuses on *algorithmic impact assessment*. Figure out what that means.
	- Is this model gonna hurt people if you deploy it? What kinds of harms are going to happen to people?
	- Canada has a more rights focus than the US. We expect Singapore to be veeery rights focused.
## Recent History of AI Summits
- Bletchely (Nov. 2023) - First major consensus on frontier risks, created modern concept of AISIs
- Seoul (May 2024) - focus on safety, created international network of AI Safety Institute. Some member states did not have a dedicated AISI but found some other organization to become a member.
	- Start of tensions between global north and global south.
- Paris (Feb. 2025) - Shift from *safety* to *action*. Increasing pressure to balance safety and innovation.
- Delhi (Feb. 2026) - hasn't happened yet! Shift from action to impact - on people, planet, progress.
	- People are worried that companies are going to dominate this one and turn it into an industry expo.

## Why do we need AISIs?
- Progress in this field is very fast, but government typically moves slow.
- Government lacks expertise. It's very hard to hire technical experts to government jobs. Most countries have it even worse than the US!
- If you are going to regulate via voluntary commitment, regulators need to figure out how to get companies to actually comply.
### How do AISIs solve this?
- Puts experts in policymaking positions!
- ... but doesn't make them bound by federal government.
- Creates trust from both government and corporations.

> [!info] Ideological disagreements between countries focus on: how do we balance innovation and safety?
- The US is a poster child of this, but a lot of other countries focus on this.
	- Rebranded AISI to Center of AI Safety and Innovation (CAISI)
	- CAISI's research is more focused on how we keep our innovative edge, especially protecting our models from geopolitical enemies (Russia and China)
	- They're no longer important...
- Before 2025, conversation was driven by safety researchers.
- Developing countries actually lean more accelerationists - they don't want to be burdened with the costs of regulatory compliance.

# Policy Considerations
- How much can we rely on companies to follow voluntary regulation?
	- Japan is actually at the forefront of making effective binding regulations that don't impede innovations.
- Access and Transparency Challenges
	- AISIs need privileged access to propietary AI models. Being part of the government give them the legitimacy to do that, but AI companies aren't thrilled about it.
	- Open question: can other countries get access to models developed in the US? What about models that are classified?
	- Who audits the models? Is it people in the AISI? Is it third party consultants?

- China has a lot of laws on the books to protect citizens from algorithmic bias, discrimination, etc. But the government is exempt from all these laws.

- A lot of interesting things are happening in LATAM in terms of regional coordination. 


- The risk conversation with AI is very different than other technical spaces - negative impacts are much more *context specific*, and it's very hard to do something about it. 
	- Ex: Say you want to prevent discriminatory outcomes form AI. Over what dimensions? Different countries worry about different types of discrimination.
- We decided that compute is how we talk about risk. But narrow, highly specialized models tend to pose the highest risk!
