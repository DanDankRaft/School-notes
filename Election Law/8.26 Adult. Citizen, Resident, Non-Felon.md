# Women's Vote
- Why did women won the vote?
	- Incentivize women to move to rural states w low female population.
	- There was also a sense of social equality in the west that didn't exist in the east.
	- AFL started supporting women's suffrage around 1910
	- WWI
		- Alexander Keyssar - voting rights tend to extend during wartime. See also Vietnam war and 26th amendment.
	- Connection with Temperance movement - women already had organizational infrastructure.
		- In many cases, women were more literate and better educated than men.
	- Part of larger Progressive Era push for democratization
- Interesting point - if you're a woman suffragette, you can try over and over again, but you only need to win once. Once you win, it's practically impossible to take your vote away.
- Only a third of ex-confederate states ratified the 19th amendment.

- 19th amendment presumes that the right to vote is something that already exists, and has the provision that invites Congress to legislate in order to enforce this amendment.
	- Open Q: What *can* Congress do using this power?
		- Narrow conception: merely ban laws or create a cause of action for governments that ban women's right to vote
		- Broader conception: use it as a gateway to pass laws enforcing broader gender equality.
			- Ex: tack aspects of social inequality that indirectly affect women's right to vote, like hours when polls are open, or requirements for vote-by-mail, or women's access to transportation.
	- One theory: once women gained the right to vote, it renders every law passed beforehand suspect, because it was passed without ever receiving the democratic consent of women - who now make up the majority of the electorate!
# Voting Age
- Why should 16yos be allowed to vote?
	- They are more integrated into their community and have a more stable life, which makes them more likely to establish the habit of voting. Vs. 18yos who are moving out to college.
	- The voting age is a historical accident.
		- Originally 21 bc of medieval British rule
		- Lowered to 18 bc that's when you can get drafted to the military
	- The parts of their brains involved in voting are fully developed.
	- 16yos are old enough to be incarcerated as adults!
- Arguments against:
	- Many of them haven't taken a civics class yet
	- They don't have enough life experience to fully weigh long-term political decisions.
	- When you live with your parents you don't have the full independence to make these kinds of decisions for your own.
		- What if your parent refuses to drive you to the polls if you disagree with their opinion?

- In many states that do caucuses, party policy determines the minimum voting age rather than state law.

- NY Court of Appeals invalidated this under the state constitution.
- Historically, noncitizen parents could vote in school board elections!
- Noncitizen voting in federal elections is illegal under federal law.

	- Non-citizen residents are counted in the census for apportionment of representatives. It's kind of weird to count them without letting them vote.
- Arguments against:
	- Citizens have more of a stake than noncitizens. That's what citizenship
	- Recent arrivals are more prone to ethnic politics
	- Politicians could recruit noncitizens to move to the US in order to vote for them...
	- It's a powerful incentive for people to become citizens and bring their families with them. Especially green card holders

# Dunn v. Blumstein - Durational Requirements
405 US 330 (1972)
- Tennessee required one-year residence in the state and **three-month residence in the county** for voting
	- At the time, most states had similar provisions.
- Law prof. who moved to his university recently challenged the law.
## Legal Q
- Does a minimum residency requirement violate Equal Protection Clause?
## Holding
- Provision unconstitutional! 6-1
- This is discrimination between old residents and new residents, which raises Equal Protection.
- We should apply **strict scrutiny**
	- The Right to Vote is a Fundamental Right. *See* *Reynolds v. Sims*
	- We're changing our mind from previous cases
	- **Selective Distribution of the Franchise is problematic** - purely telling some people they're allowed to vote but not others is always subject to strict scrutiny.

- Applying the test:
	- Compelling interest:
		- There used to be a problem with people crossing state lines in order to vote, but that's not an issue nowadays.
	- Narrowly tailored:
		- Modern voter registration systems already do that! And Tennessee already has one of those!
		- Why are you OK with a year for some contexts and 3 months for others? It seems like the duration of this requirement is independent of how long it takes you to actually verify someone's residency.
		- See also: 1970 Amendments to Voting Rights Act § 202 banned closing voter registration more than 30 days for Pres. and VP elections.
			- If every state can abide by this provision, then it doesn't need more than 30 days to verify someone's residency.
			- What power enables this: Electors Clause.
				- Electors Clause is what gives Congress the ability to regulate Pres. and VP elections. SCOTUS has read it to include Time Place and Manner regulations!

- See also *Carrington v. Rash* - important case we're going to read later when TX denied the vote to temporarily stationed soldiers.

- Alternate argument: durational residence requirements further goal of having a knowledgeable electorate. Making sure people are familiar enough with local politics and have a stake in it in order to make informed decision. 
	- Ex: letting students participate in local elections in college towns could completely outvote the long-term residents.
	- Marshall doesn't buy that.
	- Most long-time residents aren't particularly informed about their local politics either!

- Candidacy election requirements: After this case, most states require a candidate to be a resident for at least 30 days before filing to run for office.
	- Shouldn't you have strong ties to the area in order to represent it in elected office?
	- But shouldn't we let the electorate decide if they care about that?
	- But, isn't it an infringement on the right to vote to not let people vote for certain candidates?
		- Isn't it an infringement on the right to vote to not let the electorate decide who gets to run? Do we want to let Elon Musk run for office in TX?

# Felons' Right to Vote
- Should Felons be allowed to vote?
	- Arguments For:
		- They've already paid the price!
		- Voting is a fundamental right.
		- Felony is a highly variable category. Most felons are like, low-profile drug users.
			- It gives the legislature the power to disenfranchise whoever they want.
	- Arguments Against:
		- They've violated the social contract
		- "It would be crazy to let mafiosos vote for their prosecutors"
		- We want to encourage legal behavior
- Should different types of offenses be considered differently?
	- Opinions might differ between statutory categorizations (Class A Felonies) and common law felonies.
- Is there a path to reform on this?
	- One approach - target state constitutions. Certain state supreme courts might be amenable in this.
	- In Florida, a citizen initiative allowed felons to vote!
## Richardson v. Ramirez
418 US 24 (1974)
- Sued state of California from prohibiting felons from voting
- Relied on Equal Protection Clause AND 14th Amendment § 2:
> ... But when the right to vote at any election .... is denied to any of the male inhabitants of such State, bieng twenty-one years of age, and citizens of the United States, or in any way abridged, **except for participation in rebellion, or other crime**, the basis of representation \[of the state in Congress] shall be reduced ...
- Argument: Any reading of this clause that allows states to take away felons' vote is contrary to the equal protection clause, and so such readings must be incorrect
### Legal Q
- Does 14A § 2 allow states to prohibit felons from voting?
### Holding
- Yes.
> [!info] Holding
> States can prohibit felons from voting,
> unless the felons were convicted under a discriminatory law that violates the Equal Protection Clause.

- Legislative history:
	- Readmitted confederate states had to submit their constitutions to Congress for approval. One of the conditions for approval: no provisions that would take away anyone's vote, **except as punishment for such crimes as are now felonies at common law**, "whereof they shall have been duly convicted, under laws equally applicable to all the inhabitants of said State"
		- IE, except as punishment for a felony, unless the law under which they were convicted is itself racially discriminatory.
	- State-level bans on felon disenfranchisement were common in the era immediately after the 14th amendment was ratified.
		- BUT, at the time they only considered *felonies at common law*, which is different from our much broader list of modern statutory felonies.
- Tradition based argument: this has been around forever. If you want relief, go to your state legislature.

- Is there a tension between the Equal Protection Clause of § 1 and this provision of § 2?
	- Rehnquist thinks that there is. He just thinks that § 2 takes precedent over § 1.
	- Another reading: taking the right to vote for committing a felony is not a violation of equal protection - at least not by itself.
## Notes
1: 1970s SCOTUS was not an originalist court. It turns to legislative intent bc it already sees a plain textual reading and wants to verify it.
3: What are the common arguments against enfranchising felons?
- Themes grounded in American political thought:
	- Ex-felons have broken the social contract
	- Only the virtuous can participate in governing society
- There's a conflict of interest in allowing criminals to vote for those who legislate and enforce the criminal laws.
	- Kind of hinges on the notion that most felons are repeat criminals, or that criminality is part of their identity (ie, *mafiosos*)
	- "Like insane people," have raised questions about their ability to vote responsibly
4: The earliest felon disenfranchisment laws came in the late 18th/early 19th centuries, but most states adopted such laws in the 1860s.... curious...
## Hunter v. Underwood (1985)
- Alabama Constitution included provision disenfranchising individuals convicted of "any crime involving moral turpitude"
	- This included many misdemeanors
- Holding: struck down *unanimously*
	- Legislative history shows that the goal of this provision was to disenfranchise black people. They selected crimes believed to be committed more frequently by black people.
	- Even in 1985, black Alabamans were more significantly likely than whites to be disenfranchised for minor offenses.

# How Women Won the Vote
- Early states and countries that gave women the vote were in rough countryside and had relatively few women.
	- They gave women the vote to attract them to move to the state, increasing its population
	- Men there didn't fear that women would outvote them bc there were more men.
- The stories of black suffrage and women's suffrage have early differences and parallels
	- Politicians were more eager to grant them vote than the public
	- 15th amendment motivated the women's suffrage movement & provided a template for its end goal - a constitutional amendment
- Suffrage movement had strong links with democratic reforms of the Progressive Era
	- suffragettes took advantage of state initiatives, referendums and direct primaries to push for suffrage. They linked the issues together.
- Once federal lawmakers in some states started relying on women voters to get re-elected, they had to run in favor of expanding suffrage!
	- Alexander Keyssar - the suffrage endgame.
- Suffrage accelerated bc of WWI.
	- Would be seen as hypocritical to fight to make the world safe for democracy while not having democracy at home.

# Age Discrimination In Voting At Home
- June 2020 article
- In several states, voters must provide a valid excuse to vote by mail. In seven states, age is one such excuse. This is age discrimination, and a violation of the 26th amendment!
- History of 26th amendment
	- Introduced in every Congress since 1942, but didn't gain steam until the civil rights era.
	- Legislative history: "overwhelming influence" from the Fourteenth Amendment.
	- Directly state that forcing young voters to undertake special burdens like in **obtaining absentee ballots** was a violation of equal protection!
- Early 26A litigation applied strict scrutiny to age discrimination in voting!
- But since the late 1970s, 26A litigation has been non-existent...
- A lot of vote by mail laws are *prima facie* discriminatory, subject to strict scrutiny
- SCOTUS: there is no fundamental right to vote by mail, but it said that in 1969... before 26A and before COVID
- If a law does not impose a "severe" burden on voting, the courts apply a less strict test, taken from *Anderson v. Celebrezze* and *Burdick v. Takushi*
	- Article argues that *during covid*, vote by mail restrictions are "severe" burdens
- In Florida, a state where anyone can vote by mail:
	- A majority of mail-in ballots were from voters under 65
	- But ballots from younger voters were rejected for irregularities at higher rates.
- In states that require an excuse to vote by mail, all cohorts vote by mail at lower rates than the national average - even 65+!!!
# In Defense of Lowering the Voting Age
- Article from 2016!
	- Notes how several municipalities and countries(!) already lowered the voting age to 16!
- Our initial voting age of 21 was a leftover from Britain. Our best theory: 21 was when a man was old enough to wear a suit of knightly armor.
- The 26th amendment does not forbid lowering the voting age under 18. It just prohibits raising it to above 18.
- 16-17yos often have higher turnout than 18-22yos in the same election.
	- This is because the barriers to registering to vote as a 16yo are lower than an 18yo who is dealing with moving + absentee ballots for the first time...
- 16yos have some social obligations that would justify giving them the vote:
	- They can drive, and must obey traffic laws
	- They can work
	- They at the age of majority (Consent)
	- They can voluntarily drop out of school!
- Their brains are developed enough to make the decision-making necessary to vote.
- Common argument against 16yos (they will just give their parents an extra vote) is identical to the argument made against women's suffrage

# Dunn v. Blumstein
405 US 330 (1972)
- Tennessee required one-year residence in the state and **three-month residence in the county** for voting
- College prof. who moved to his university recently challenged the law.
## Legal Q
- Does a minimum residency requirement violate Equal Protection Clause?
## Holding
- Provision unconstitutional!
- Durational residence requirements for *voting* are measured by strict scrutiny.
- If this was merely a *bona fide* residence requirement would've been fine.
- Of note: Voting Rights Act § 202 banned durational residence requirements for Pres. and VP elections
	- The fact that this matters to strict scrutiny indicates a living constitutional approach...
- Applying the test:
	- There is a compelling government interest: preventing outsiders from manipulating outcome of local elections.
	- NOT narrowly tailored: modern voter registration systems already do that! And Tennessee already has one of those!
	- TN would still have enough time to verify someone's residence if they only had 30 days, instead of 3 months.
- Alternate argument: durational residence requirements further goal of having a knowledgeable electorate
	- also met by *bona fide* residence requirement.
