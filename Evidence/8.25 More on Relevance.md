# Alpha v. Hooper
- Hooper, a cop, shot Alpha to death. He argues qualified immunity, which requires his actions to be objectively reasonable under the circumstances, based on information that he knew at the time.
- Hooper's suspicion: Alpha was on meth.
- Evidence D wanted to admit: autopsy V had meth on him at the time?
- 5th circuit: can admit that.
# Sherrod v. Barry
- Police officer says "I shot him bc I thought he was making a move to pull out a gun"
- Evidence P wanted to admit: that a search found he didn't have a gun.
- 7th circuit: can't admit that.

- It's really hard to reconcile these two cases. The cynical read: courts are more likely to be sympathetic to the cops.

- Different chains of inferences are the difference between admissibility and inadmissibility - moreso than the quality of the evidence itself!

# Rule 401 Test for Relevant Evidence
> Evidence is relevant if:
> 	(a) if it has **any tendency** to make a fact **more or less probable** than it would be without the evidence; and
- IE, does it have some **incremental impact**.
$$P(\text{Fact} | \text{Evidence}) \ne P(\text{Evidence}) $$
> 	(b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action
- IE, **materiality**
$$ P(\text{Verdict} | \text{Fact}) \ne P(\text{Verdict}) $$
## Materiality
- Wesley Snipes case: in trial for failure to pay income tax, he argued that he did it bc of bad advice
	- Is this relevant? Depends on whether the tax code requires intent. **That's materiality!!**
- Texas Penal Code: intoxication means: "not having the normal use of mental pr physical faculties of reason... **or** having an alcohol concentration of 0.08 or more"
	- D1: prosecution already proved concentration of 0.12, but D wants to offer evidence that they recited the alphabet backwards and could walk in a straight line. **Not material** bc the prosecution's legal theory already proved something that is sufficient to determine the outcome.
	- D2: prosecution offers no BAC evidence. Instead, testifies that D2 reeked of alcohol and slurred her words. D2 offers the same evidence. **Material** because it directly refutes a fact that could change the outcome based on the substantive law.

# Simon v. Town of Kennebunkport
- Sidewalk case - judge refused to admit evidence that witnesses saw other people trip on that sidewalk
- Q: how do we know that the judge refused to admit this evidence, if it's not on the record?
	- Offer of Proof - put it on the record that the party offered some proof so that there's a record for appeal in case it gets rejected.
- Holding: the evidence can be admitted, but first the judge has to find that the other falls happened in substantially similar circumstances.
	- Why this matters? the case was in Maine, where it can be really snowy. If the falls happened when the sidewalk was icy, that doesn't say much about the condition of the sidewalk.
- Holding: even then, evidence may still be excluded by FRE 403, which states it may be outweighed by any number of "countervailing considerations"
	- The court was worried about potentially calling in 10 different witnesses to testify to this thing.
	- Here, the court wasn't worried about that too much, because P only wanted to call in two witnesses.
### Rule 103 - Preserving a Claim of Error
> **(a) Preserving a Claim of Error** A party may claim error in a ruling to admit or exclude evidence only if the error affects a substantial right of the party and:
> 	(1) if the ruling admits evidence, a party, on the record:
> 		(A) timely objects or moves to strike; and
> 		(B) states the specific ground, unless it was apparent from the context; or
> 	(2) if the ruling excludes evidence, a party informs the court of its substance by an offer of proof, unless the substance was apparent from the context.
> (c) **Court's statement about the ruling, directing an offer of proof.** The court must allow a party to make an offer of proof outside the jury's presence as soon as practicable- and before the court reads its charge to the jury.
> **IN TEXAS:** At a party's request, the court must direct that an offer of proof be made in question-and-answer form
> Or the court may do so on its own

## Rule 403 - Excluding Relevant Evidence for Prejudice, Confusion, Waste of Time, Other Reasons
> The court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following:
> 	unfair prejudice,
> 	confusing the issues,
> 	misleading the jury,
> 	undue delay,
> 	wasting time,
> 	or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence.
$$|P(\text{Fact}|E_1) - P(\text{Fact})| > \text{CountervailingConsiderations}(E_1) $$
- If you have nothing else to object to, lawyers will always pull out Rule 403
# United States v. James
- James's daughter ended up killing her boyfriend, using a gun that James gave to her daughter. James's argument is that she gave the daughter the gun for use in self-defense, and she knew that her bf was problematic...
- Her daughter told her that the boyfriend:
	- killed someone
	- stabbed someone w/ pen
	- beat man w/ car mirror
	- robbed old man, used knife
	- Raped the mother
- District Court excluded:
	- **Documentation** verifying that he robbed old man, used knife, had 38 priors
	-  James didn't know of the *documentation* when she gave the gun. Not what was known at the time.
- Appellate court **reversed**
- Prosecution's argument:
	- We already heard so much stuff about James, this doesn't have much marginal probative value atp...
- D's argument:
	- It makes it more likely that she heard the stories, more believable at the time
	- Adds richness to the story, makes her story more credible.
	- Problem: doesn't add a lot of probative value, creates 403 considerations!
		- Unfair prejudice? After all they already heard, doesn't change very much.

# Hypo - Embezzlement
- D on trial for embezzlement from employer.
	- P offers evidence that D had been accused of:
		- Shoplifting
		- Writing bad check
		- Stealing credit card
- Chain of inference:
	- D accused of these -> D did these -> D has pattern/character consistent with embezzlement -> D embezzled
	- If these accusations would make you trust a person less, than it's relevant.
	- It's probably **relevant,** but still excluded for **unfair prejudice**
- **Unfair prejudice** - accused != convicted

- Why we're worried about unfair prejudice:
	- maybe these accusations are true, maybe they're not. But we might not trust the jury to figure that out
	- They're not going to appropriately appreciate the difference between an accusation and a conviction.

**Forms of Unfair Prejudice**
- Putting more weight on some evidence than it actually merits
- Jury will confuse the issues. Substitute the other crimes for this one.
- Third form - probability matrix below
### Expected Outcome of Jury Regret
- Imagine that the jury tries to minimize Regret units.
- Jury decides right (votes for P & D is guilty OR votes for D and D is innocent) = 0 Regretis
- Jury decides wrong (votes for P & D is innocent OR votes for D and D is guilty) = 10 Regretis
- Imagine that after hearing all the evidence, the jury sees a *probability* that D is guilty.
- Jury sees *expected outcome* of regretting their decision and votes accordingly.
$$ E(D) = P(D) \times \text{Regretis} $$
- In cases where P(D) = 0.5, our tiebreaker is *preponderance of the evidence
$$\begin{align}
\text{Preponderence of the Evidence: } \\
P(D) > 0.5 \rightarrow D \text{ wins} \\
P(D) = 0.5 \rightarrow D \text{ wins} \\
P(D) < 0.5 \rightarrow P \text{ wins}
\end{align}$$

- **What if you have a 50-50 case where the D's insurance company pays the settlement**
- The jury would feel less regret in wrongfully deciding *for* the plaintiff.
- Jury decides wrong *for the plaintiff* = 5 Regretis
$$E(D) = P(D) \times 10$$
$$ E(P) = P(P) \times 5$$
$$\begin{align}
\text{How Juries Act When D can't pay: } \\
P(D) > 0.5 \rightarrow D \text{ wins} \\
P(D) = 0.5 \rightarrow P \text{ wins} \\
P(D) < 0.5 \rightarrow P \text{ wins}
\end{align}$$
- **Any evidence that makes the jury feel less regret for an erroneous decision in favor of the plaintiff is unfair prejudice**
	- Ex: that Plaintiff is a great person
	- Ex: that D has insurance

- Common summary: evidence has unfair prejudice because it pushes the jury to make a decision on an emotional, rather than rational basis.
