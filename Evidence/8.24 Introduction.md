- Federal Rules of Evidence
- The hardest part of evidence is Hearsay. And it has a ton of exceptions.
- Another complex part - witnesses!
- We'll only focus on TRE insofar that they're different from the FRE
- Important principles of evidence:
	- Cases have to be decided on a timely manner
	- Evidence admitted has to be perceived by the public as accurate!
		- Perception of fairness and accuracy > actual fairness and accuracy.
	- Extrinsic social policy
		- Ex: you can't admit evidence of someone taking remedial measures after an accident, in order to not discourage people from making remedial measures.

## History of FRE
- First enacted in 1975.
- Well into the 1970s, evidence was a common law subject in most courts!
- In 1973, SCOTUS forwarded to Congress a proposal for the FRE based on the Rules Enabling Act.
	- They did not get enacted that year because of Watergate!!! US v. Nixon!
- Eventually, the House voted on a different set of FRE than the ones SCOTUS submitted.
- The FRE have **extensive legislative history**. Appendix B in textbook.
	- Incredibly useful for understanding what a rule means!
	- Take precedence over the actual rules
- Rewritten in 2011 - non-substantive restyling.
	- TRE were similarly restyled in 2015
- A lot of our cases use the pre-restyling FRE.

- 45 states adopted rules based on the FRE
	- California and Nevada? had codified rules before the FRE
	- Massachusetts, New York - no formally codified rules, but there are non-binding guidelines that are very similar
	- Missouri - good luck!

# Relevance
- Irrelevant evidence is not admissible.
- Another reading "relevant evidence is admissible, unless..."

- Textbook hypo
	- Donald sued in wrongful death suit, alleged that he shot Victim because he was in love with Victim's wife.
	- Donald wrote a love letter to Wanda
- Is the love letter admissible?
	- Assumptions we make:
		- If Donald wrote the letter that means he loves Wanda. Probability: very likely
		- If Donald loves Wanda that means he wants her for himself. Probability: somewhat lower
		- If Donald wants her for himself, that means he wants to get rid of Victor
		- If Donald wants to get rid of Victor, that means he wants to kill him
		- If he wants to kill Victor, that means he killed him.
- Is the letter relevant? yea. But it doesn't really prove he killed Victor.
- Eyewitness evidence: direct evidence that someone saw Donald shoot Victor
- It's not necessary that evidence proves the proposition, merely that it *tends to* prove the proposition.

- **Direct evidence** - doesn't require inferences from the evidence to the conclusion.
- **Circumstantial evidence** - requires inferences from the evidence to the conclusion.

- Direct evidence is usually better than circumstantial evidence but not always.
	- Ex: DNA evidence is the best type of evidence, and it's purely circumstantial!

- Courts typically don't give jury instructions about circumstantial vs direct evidence.
- Most often: lawyer will attack opposing counsel's evidence by calling it circumstantial.

- **Conditional relevancy**:
	- Book says "the love letter is *allegedly* written by Donald."
	- Authentication problem: in order to admit the letter into evidence, you have to prove that Donald wrote the letter!

## Conditional Evidence
- There are situations where multiple separate pieces of evidence are irrelevant by themselves, but become relevant in combination
	- Person was a Taylor Swift fan
	- There was a Taylor Swift concert in the vicinity
- **FRE 104(b) Relevance That Depends on a Fact** - evidence may be admitted on the condition that its relevance will be proven later on.
	- The judge has a lot of discretion on if/how to allow such evidence.
- **FRE 611(a)** - judges generally have a lot of discretion on the mode and order of presenting evidence

# Knapp
- D shot and killed an officer attempting to arrest him.
- D argued self defense: he heard that someone else was beaten to death by this cop and that reasonably scared him.
- Prosecution offered evidence: that other person died of senility and alcoholism
- D's response: the story doesn't have to be true. What matters is that he believed that was true.
## Legal Q
- Is evidence to the truth of a story relevant to whether someone believed that story? 
## Holding
- Evidence of senility and alcoholism admitted.
- We presume that people will generally tell the truth to each other and not lie.
- Evidence that a story is false tends to evidence that D hadn't heard that story.
