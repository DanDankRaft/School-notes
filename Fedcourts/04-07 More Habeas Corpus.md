# Burt v. Tiltow
571 US 12 (2013)
Applying the *Strickland* standard.

- "Double deferential" standard - deference to both **judge** and **defense attorney**. Give them benefit of the doubt!
- Under §2254(d)(1), this means you have to prove that the state court applied *Strickland* in an "objectively unreasonable manner"

# Renico v. Lett
559 US 766 (2010)
- Defendant's first trial was a mistrial, resulted in deadlocked jury. Convicted in second trial.
- In state petition for habeas, D argued that the first trial was improperly declared a mistrial.

## Holding
- Petition denied!
- This is a rule where state judges are owed a ton of discretion, so we are going to give them leeway here.

# Adjudication-on-the-merits
- 2254(d) requires deference with respect to any claim that "was adjudicated on the merits in State court proceedings"
# Harrington v. Richter
562 US 86 (2011)
- State court issued an order without an opinion explaining their reasoning.
## Holding
- This is upheld as a valid adjudication on the merits.
- "The statute only refers to a decision, which resulted from an adjudication"
- Requiring states to explain *why* they arrived at any decision could undermine the integrity of their case-law tradition

# Johnson v. Williams
568 U.S. 289 (2013)
- Petitioner raised both state and federal claims, but the court decision against him only issued an opinion that addressed the state claims
## Holding
- This is also a valid adjudication on the merits! If it's fine when they issue no opinion, it's fine if they only issue an opinion on some of the claims.

- Scholars tend to think that federal judges are more vigorous and intentional in enforcing federal rights than state judges.
- Some proposed reasons:
	- Federal judges are more competent - smaller pool, harder to become one, appointed not elected
	- Federal judges pay closer attention to federal tradition and the constitution - feel more accountable to SCOTUS
	- Federal judges are less tied to where they're seated. While this means they're less in tune with local social conditions, it also means federal litigation is more focused on *federal law* than trying to appease the particular judge or community.
