# Edwards v. Vannoy
593 US __ (2021)
## Background
 - Petitioner was convicted by 11/12 jury members.
 - 2015: He filed a federal habeas petition, arguing that the constitution required a unanimous jury conviction.
 - 2020: *Ramos v. Louisiana* held that the constitution required a unanimous jury for criminal conviction.
	 - Decided while his writ for certoriari was pending in SCOTUS
## Legal Question
- Does *Ramos* apply retroactively to overturn his conviction on a federal habeas review of his state case?
## Plurality Holding (Kavanauh)
- No.
- Old test from *Teague*:
	1. Did the case in question (*Ramos*) announce a new rule of criminal procedure?
	2. If it did announce a new rule, is it a "watershed" rule so significant that it should be applied retroactively?
- Kavanaugh: based on subsequent precedent, the watershed requirement is so high that  no rule will ever meet it. This is the most important, bedrock, foundational rule ever and it still doesn't meet the watershed requirements.
- Stare decisis consideration: because of this high bar, nobody reasonably relies on *Teague* to overturn their conviction.
- Conclusion: ***Teague* is overturned**. No new rule applies retroactively in federal review of habeas petitions.
## Dissent (Kagan)
- This is absolutely a watershed rule!
- This rule is so important that, in *Ramos*, it justified overturning a really old precedent!
- Non-unanimous juries were explicitly created as a tool of racial injustice - everyone agrees on that! Even the plurality!
- It makes sense that there are tons of cases that didn't find "watershed" rules - that's what happens when the court creates a requirement that's deliberately narrow by design!
- This goes against stare decisis: nobody here asked us to overturn *Teagle*, and the reasoning here is sloppy.
## Gorsuch Concurrence
- Historically, habeas petitions were only awarded if:
	- A person was detained for no reason, or
	- The convicting court had no jurisdiction over this case.
- Gorsuch thinks federal collateral habeas review should only be used if the convicting court lacked jurisdiction!!
	- Note: a court might lack jurisdiction if it failed to properly apply a new "substantive" rule of federal constitutional law.
	- Depending on how far you take this, that might expand habeas jurisdiction beyond the AEDPA...
## Thomas Concurrence
 - This constitutional debate doesn't matter.
 - AEDPA, by itself, does not allow us to retroactively apply a new federal constitutional rule.
 - How can a new rule that was not well-established at the time of trial possibly be "contrary to ... clearly established Federal law"? How can applying the old rule at the time possibly be "an unreasonable application of clearly established Federal law"?
