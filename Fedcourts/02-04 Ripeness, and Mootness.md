# Review from Last Class
- Third party standing - a prudential doctrine.
	- It makes it *harder* to have your day in court. You might have typical Article III standing and TP Standing might still make you lose!
	- Court is weary of adjudicating somebody else's rights.
- Requirements for third party standing:
	- Plaintiff must have typical Article III standing
	- Some close relationship between plaintiff and third party, s.t. the third party relies on the plaintiff to exercise its right - like doctor-client, buyer-seller.
		- May be weakened if the relationship is hypothetical - like an attorney suing for the right of potential indigent defendants
	- Obstacle making it harder for the third party to assert rights.
		- Can be a legal or practical obstacle
		- Obstacle doesn't need to be super big. It just needs to be *hard*, not *impossible* to bring suit.

- Best way to think of Third Party standing: its a rebuttable presumption.

# Ripeness
## O'Shea v. Littleton
414 U.S. 488 (1974)
### Background
- Plaintiff claimed that D, a judge, repeatedly engaged in illegal and discriminatory bond-setting and sentencing against her.
- P sued to get equitable relief preventing the judge from doing that again.
### Legal Q
- Is there standing to prevent future instances of a some past injury?
### Holding
- No.
- If P will be again arrested and charged and sentenced in an illegal manner, then they can bring claims. But they can't just grant equitable relief in anticipation that P might break the law and be arrested again!
	- This is *speculative* - long chain of causation that you might commit this crime again, you might get arrested, you might get brought by the same magistrates, you might be subject to illegal sentencing
- In other words, an injury-in-fact must be concrete, particularized, and imminent. This injury is not imminent.

> [!important] Ripeness in this case is the same as the imminence requirement for injury-in-fact.
## Los Angeles v. Lyons
561 U.S. 95 (1983)
- Lyons was unjustifiably subjected to a chokehold during a routine traffic stop which left him severely injured.
	- The infraction: a broken tail light
	- Lyons was black.
- Sued for both damages and injunctive relief.
### Legal Q
- Did he have standing for:
	- Damages?
	- Injunctive relief?
### Holding
- Only for monetary damages.
> [!important] Must show standing for each form of relief sought.
> For injunctive relief, you must show a high likelihood that you will be injured again.

- Court's POV is that this is just an individual citizen asking for an injunction against being injured again for his own sake.
	- Therefore, you can't get equitable relief unless you establish that you'll be put in a chokehold again.
> [!info] People have different understandings of social facts. When you argue before a judge or jury, you need to manage to convince someone who understands the facts differently and who watches different media.

- Mere allegation that this part of a pattern of racial discrimination from LA police is not enough to meet standing.

- Court is extremely averse to get involved in cases about state and local police action.
- Case came up to court after LAPD already implemented a moratorium on chokeholds - so SCOTUS might not have cared as much.

# Mootness
- Requirement that a live controversy exist throughout the litigation.
- IE, plaintiff is no longer suffering an injury that can be redressed.
- There are exceptions to mootness: **they only apply if the exception is moot**
- Exceptions:
	- Voluntary Cessation
	- Capable of Repetition yet Evading Review

# Voluntary Cessation of Illegal Activity
> [!info] Test: no reasonable likelihood that the wrong will recur
> The burden lies on the **defendant!!**
## United States v. W.T. Grant Co
345 U.S. 629 (1953)
- D violated antitrust law by sharing directors across competing corporations.
- During litigation, the problematic directors resigned.
- Holding: **NOT moot** - exception applies.
	- There's still a dispute over whether the challenged practices were illegal. Until this is settled, the **defendant is free to return to their old ways**.
	- There's a **public interest in having this settled.**
	- If there's no reasonable expectation that D might return to challenged practice, then the case might become moot
## Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.
568 U.S. 85 (2013)
- Nike sued Already for trademark infringement. Already counter-claimed trademark invalidity.
- Nike realized Already was going to win their claim, so they moved to dismiss their own claim with prejudice.
	- Nike signed a whole ass covenant
- Already wanted to continue the case...
- Holding: **Moot - exception does not apply.**
	- "the allegedly wrongful behavior could \[not] reasonably be expected to recur."

## Aladdin's Castle, Associated General Contractors
City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283 (1982)
Northeastern Fl. Chapter of Associated Gen. Contractors v. City of Jacksonville, 508 U.S. 656 (1993)
- In both cases, a city repealed an ordinance while it was challenged in court for being unconstitutional.
	- In *Associated Gen. Contractors*, the city repealed the ordinance and replaced it with a "more carefully worded one" that wouldn't violate the constitution.
- Holding: **NOT moot** - exception applies.
	- Nothing stopped the cities from reenacting the ordinance again.
- In *Associated Gen. Contractors* - they already repeated the offending conduct.
## New York State Rifle & Pistol Association, Inc. v. City of New York
590 U.S. ___ (2020)
- NYC ordinance placed restrictions on where handguns can be used.
- Plaintiffs sued for violation of 2nd amendment
- Lower courts rule in favor of the city, but then SCOTUS grants certiorari. Subsequently, NYC amended the ordinance to allow the offending conduct. **And NY state passed a law preventing NYC from changing their mind.**
	- What was NYC thinking? Oh shit we're gonna lose before SCOTUS and set a terrible standing. Get this out of court NOW.
- Holding: **Moot** - exception does not apply.
	- This is bc *the state* passed the law preventing the plaintiff from re-enacting the bad law.

# "Capable of Repetition, Yet Evading Review"
- This issue has been mooted by the passage of time, but is likely to come up again between the same parties.
	- Reasonable expectation or demonstrated probability
	- Must be the same *issue*
	- With respect to the same *plaintiff*
	- And the same *defendant*

- Common case capable of repetition yet evading review - abortion.
	- Pregnancy lasts 9 months. There's no way the case is gonna reach SCOTUS before the baby is born.
	- But this will probably repeat - what if Roe gets pregnant again and wants an abortion.
	- This is exactly what SCOTUS says in Roe v. Wade.
## First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti
435 U.S. 765 (1978)
- Campaign finance law prevented businesses from making certain expenditures on elections.
- Corporations sued over a specific referendum on taxes. By the time it reached SCOTUS, the referendum happened and failed.
- Holding: **NOT moot**  - capable of repetition yet evading review.
	- The legislature tried proposing this issue many times before. But each time, the referendum failed before the law could be properly reviewed.
## Honing v. Doe
484 U.S. 305 (1988)
- "stay-put" provision of the education act - disabled child shall remain in his or her current educational placement.
- Plaintiff's child was expelled for disruptive conduct coming out of their emotional disability. By this point, the child has left the public school system entirely.
- Holding: **NOT moot** - capable of repetition yet evading review.
	- While the child was not currently in school, they still had an emotional disability, so this issue would inevitably return.
- This is a relatively generous application of this precedent, but it still shows how lenient the court tends to be - they're assuming that 

## DeFunis v. Odegaard
416 U.S. 312 (1974)
- Plaintiff was a white guy rejected from UW law school. Sued over unconstitutional racial quotas.
- DeFunis won at lower level and got admitted to the school.
- By the time case reached SCOTUS, DeFunis was on his last semester and everyone agreed that regardless of the outcome, he would still be able to continue attending.
- Holding: **Moot** - not capable of repetition yet evading review.
	- The student would graduate no matter what. This case was not capable of repetition wrt this plaintiff.
- Dissent: we're doing the public a *disservice.* This is a hot-button constitutional question, and it is inevitable that it'll come back to us soon.
> [!info] SCOTUS 

- When SCOTUS finds a federal case moot, they vacate the ruling to a lower court and tell them to dismiss the case.
	- They don't want the intermediate court's decision to become a binding precedent that cannot be reviewed.
- When SCOTUS vacates a circuit court decision, it makes a very deliberate decision to pick one of very many appellate court decisions and getting rid of precedents. **Granting certoriai and dismissing a case for mootness is actually a very powerful tool**
