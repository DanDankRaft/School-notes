# The Doctrine
- SCOTUS does not have jurisdiction to review state supreme court decisions that:
	- rest on an **adequate** proposition of state law (IE, does not conflict with any preempting federal law); and
	- where the state's reasoning is **independent** of federal law (IE, the outcome wasn't determined by federal law)
- There's scholarly debate whether this rule is:
	- Constitutionally required
	- A procedural rule SCOTUS sets on itself; or
	- A prudential rule

# Michigan v. Long
463 U.S. 1032 (1983)

## Background
- Issue: can a police officer do a protective search on someone's car without a warrant, so long that they have an "articulable suspicion" that they were armed and dangerous?
- Michigan Supreme Court said no.

## Legal Question
- Does SCOTUS have jurisdiction to review this case?

## Holding
- Yes, it does!
- The test for whether a decision is not "independent":
	- court does not give "plain statement" that their decision is adequate and independent; and
	- the decision appears to rest primarily on federal law
- If that's the case, then we presume that the state court decided the case bc it thought federal law required it to do so.

- Here: court opinion almost exclusively rests on federal law.

- Why it's good policy: doesn't require SCOTUS to interpret state laws!

- Dissent: we used to presume that adequate state grounds are independent until proven otherwise. Today, we are changing it to a presumption that they're *not* independent!
