# Review from Last Class
- Political Question Doctrine
	- Textually demonstrable commitment: If we look at the text of the constitution, does it say that a political branch should resolve the question, and not the court;
	- Judicially manageable standard: there a legal standard or test that we can adopt here that makes sense; and
	- Prudential - is this an issue that the court should even decide? (*Goldwater*)

- In practice, SCOTUS very rarely finds that something is a political question. The cases we studied in this class are basically every single time the court found a political question since *Baker v. Carr*.
- BUT, lower courts find political questions all the time. One theory: if SCOTUS doesn't wanna get involved, it can just deny cert.

- This doctrine is quite frustrating, depending on how you read it, either everything is a political question or nothing is a political question. So, when is it taken seriously in litigation?
	- When there's an ongoing fight in Congress about this issue.
	- When we're talking about elections
	- In foreign affairs
	- Guarantee clause (federal government shall ensure to each state a republican form of government)

# The Independent and Adequate State Law Ground Doctrine
- SCOTUS does not have jurisdiction to review state supreme court decisions that:
	- rest on an **adequate** proposition of state law (IE, does not conflict with any preempting federal law); and
	- where the state's reasoning is **independent** of federal law (IE, the outcome wasn't determined by federal law)
- There's scholarly debate whether this rule is:
	- Constitutionally required
	- A procedural rule SCOTUS sets on itself; or
	- A prudential rule

- No SCOTUS appellate jurisdiction if there's a separate adequate and independent state law grounds.
	- **Adequate** - is it a real rule of state law.
		- Created in response to reconstruction-era cases where southern courts suddenly got really nitpicky about procedural state law with black plaintiffs.
	- **Independent** - the question of federal law would not have changed the outcome of the case.
- Purpose:
	- To avoid advisory opinions. If they're deciding on a case where the outcome depends on state law and not federal law, this opinion isn't binding and maybe transgresses Article III.
		- *Can* SCOTUS decision make a difference in how the case comes out. But not *will* SCOTUS decision make a difference.
	- Federalism - we want to respect the independence of state courts.
	- Efficiency - SCOTUS is so overburdened, this'll lighten the caseload!

# Michigan v. Long
463 U.S. 1032 (1983)
## Background
- Long was stopped for speeding, cops saw a knife in his car, searched his car without a warrant, and found 75 lbs of weed. He argued that it wasn't his car and he didn't know about or notice the 75 lbs of weed.
	- Why do they take it to SCOTUS? Weed was a contentious political issue in Michigan at the time, and the state AG wanted to make a statement.
- Issue: can a police officer do a protective search on someone's car without a warrant, so long that they have an "articulable suspicion" that they were armed and dangerous?
- Michigan Supreme Court said no.
## Legal Question
- Does SCOTUS have jurisdiction to review this case?
- When ruling in Long's favor, SCOMI cited the Michigan state constitution twice. Long argued that this created an adequate and independent grounds.
## Holding
- Yes, it does!
- How to find out if a state court decision is independent
	- Ask the court - too much work
	- Dig through all the state law themselves - too much work
	- Presume that it's independent - **hurts uniform enforcement of federal law**
	- Presume that it's not independent - what they do.

- Presumption:
	- When a decision **fairly appears to rest on federal law,** SCOTUS will assume that it does.
- Rebutting the presumption: a state court can rebut this presumption with a "plain statement" that they do not feel reliant on federal law.

- Arguments against the presumption:
	- This is the rare case where the court is presuming jurisdiction when it's not clear - it runs the risk of creating a ton of advisory opinions. Once this gets vacated to the lower court, they could just make this ruling dependent on state law alone.
	- Against: this means that if the court doesn't make this presumption, SCOTUS can just steal this opinion from under them. Since when does SCOTUS tell state courts how to write their opinion??
- 


- The test for whether a decision is not "independent":
	- court does not give "plain statement" that their decision is adequate and independent; and
	- the decision appears to rest primarily on federal law
- If that's the case, then we presume that the state court decided the case bc it thought federal law required it to do so.

- Here: court opinion almost exclusively rests on federal law.

- Why it's good policy: doesn't require SCOTUS to interpret state laws!

- Uniformity is good

- Dissent: we used to presume that adequate state grounds are independent until proven otherwise. Today, we are changing it to a presumption that they're *not* independent!

# What happened after *Michigan v. Long*? What does SCOTUS care about?
- Stevens' Dissent: we should not get involved in a case where the lower court already ruled in favor of the rights of the little guy.
	- It looks bad! Up until 1914, we didn't even have appellate jurisdiction in state court cases that affirmed federal rights.
- Arguments in favor: the government is hurt by decisions like these! One of the frustrations before *Michigan v. Long* was that nobody could overrule a decision by a St. Ct. that mixed state and federal law.
	- It seemed like state courts could defend criminal defendants left and right and the executive and legislature couldn't do anything about that!
- The effect of this: state courts are interpreting the rights granted by state constitutions the same as SCOTUS interpreting federal constitutional rights. Before *Michigan v. Long*, they would often find additional protections for criminal defendants coming from their state constitutions.

- Why are st. ct. judges accepting the decisions of SCOTUS as state law? **Because they're elected, and they don't want to face the consequences of going against SCOTUS to defend criminal defendants?**
