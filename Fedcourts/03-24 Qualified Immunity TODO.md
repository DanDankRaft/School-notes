# Wilson, Saucier, Callahan
- *Callahan* - federal courts don't have to decide whether a violation happened, if they can independently say that the law just wasn't clearly established. Overturned *Wilson, Saucier*

- Rationale:
	- Lower courts have to decide a gazillion cases. Why do they have to decide an issue that's completely irrelevant if they're going to side with the defendant anyways! It'd be dicta!
	- At the motion to dismiss stage, they can only consider the facts of the complaint! That'd be an entirely hypothetical ruling!
	- No precedential impact - this test is so fact-dependent!
	- Maybe the briefs are not very good!
	- Wasting resources for the *litigants!* It's a
	- Bad publicity to say - you violated the constitution but there's no remedy. Erodes court legitimacy
	- Constitutional avoidance - we don't wanna decide a constitutional claim!
	- If the court is not inclined to establish rights, forcing them to decide on the merits *shrinks* rights. Instead of saying "yes violation, not clearly established," they'll just say there was no violation.
- Arguments against:
	- Can't "clearly establish" law if you're constantly avoiding things! Especially if you don't have any precedent to look at
	- Legitimacy counterresponse - prevents us from establishing more constitutional rights!
		- The role of the courts in a democracy is to establish rights!
		- Under separation of powers - the court's job is to tell us what the law is!
	- It was never dicta! It was part of the analysis!
