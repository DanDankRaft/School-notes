# Section 1983 and Habeas
§1983:
> Every person who under color of \[state law\] ... subjects any person ... to the deprivation of rights secured by the constitution and laws ... shall be liable

AEDPA:
> Federal courts shall entertain an application for a writ of habeas corpus in behalf of a person in custody pursuant to the judgment of state court only on the ground ... in custody in violation of the constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States

- On the surface, the text of 1983 allows you to bring any claims .

## Procedural Hurdles: 1983 and Habeas compared
|                     | 1983                                                                                          | Habeas                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Custody Requirement | no need to be in custody                                                                      | Must be "in custody"                                                                                                                     |
| Exhaustion          | No exhaustion requirement (*Monroe v. Pape*)                                                  | Exhaustion requirement!                                                                                                                  |
| Proc. Default       | No procedural default problems (bc no exhaustion!)                                            | Procedural default problems                                                                                                              |
| Deference           | No need to defer to state court! The state court may not have even decided on this issue yet! | §2254(d): deference to state court! Cannot win unless its contrary to or an unreasonable application of cleraly established federal law. |
| Time limit          | Statute of limitations: dependent on state law, usually longer than 1 year.                   | Time limit: 1 year!                                                                                                                      |
| Relief              | Monetary or injunctive                                                                        | Only future injunctive! Only: release, new trial, or re-sentencing                                                                       |
- Conclusion: if you can bring the same claim under habeas or 1983, you would absolutely prefer to bring it under 1983!!!
	- That's why SCOTUS said we can't read 1983 that way. Otherwise, habeas would be superfluous
# Preiser v. Rodriguez
411 U.S. 475 (1973)

> [!error] §1983 cannot be used to challenge the length of your sentence or your confinement in general

> [!success] §1983 can be used to challenge the conditions of your confinement
> E.g.: food being served, respect of religious liberty, treatment by guards, etc.
## Background
- Plaintiff was denied good-conduct-time credits as a result of disciplinary proceedings, and brought suit in federal court under §1983.
- Problem: he could've brought this same action via a habeas petition.
## Legal Question
- In situations where both §1983 and Habeas are available, does one preclude the other?
## Holding
- Yes. The federal habeas statute precludes §1983 actions in situations where:
	- The prisoner is challenging the duration of their confinement,
	- Due to constitutional violations happening once he was imprisoned (rather than the underlying criminal conviction), and
	- The remedy he seeks is a reduced sentence or immediate release.
- BUT, if the same chain of events leads to separate claims on both length of confinement and the conditions of their confinement, the prisoner can bring one under habeas and the other under §1983.

- There are policy reasons for this:
	- We want to give state courts / institutions a chance to correct their own mistakes before bringing a federal action.
		- Habeas requires exhausting state remedies, §1983 does not!
	- Younger abstention rationale - this is an area of particularly strong state interest.

- If the prisoner is seeking damages, he is attacking something other than the length of his confinement.
- Other cases that seem analogous - like a prisoner being denied access to some services because of his religious beliefs (*Cooper*) - are not about speedy or immediate release.

# Heck v. Humphrey
512 U.S. 477 (1994)

> [!info] You can sue for damages due to malicious prosecution under §1983, BUT
> You cannot bring a §1983 claim if a ruling in favor of the plaintiff would necessarily imply the invalidity of the conviction or sentence.
> So, you must prove that your conviction was invalidated or overturned *before* you bring your §1983 suit. Incl.,  habeas, expungement via executive order, not guilty verdict, reversal of conviction on direct appeal.
- Statute of limitation for §1983 doesn't start ticking until *after* your conviction is overturned!

## Background
- Plaintiff was convicted in state trial court of voluntary manslaughter
- While his direct appeal was pending, he sued the prosecutor and police investigator under § 1983, alleging that they engaged in an unlawful investigation and knowingly destroyed evidence.
	- Q: what violation is he alleging here? due process?
- He sued here for **damages**
## Legal Question
- Can a criminal defendant use §1983 to sue for damages over their pending or completed state criminal proceedings?
## Holding
- Not here.
- Analogy - common law tort of "malicious prosecution"
	- Used when you wanna sue the prosecutor for abusing their power to go after you bc they have some vendetta against you or whatever.
	- **One element of this tort is that the proceeding terminated in favor of the accused** - through a not guilty verdict, an appeal, expungement, or **writ of habeas corpus**
		- Policy justification: we don't want someone to be convicted, and then win a tort action!
- **Rule:** to win a §1983 action over unconstitutional conviction or imprisonment, a plaintiff must prove that their conviction was reversed!

## Weird Scalia Footnote
- Even despite all of this, you can still bring a §1983 claim for violating the 4th amendment, because it is entirely possible that even if the evidence is not admitted to consideration, you could've still be found guilty or sentenced.
