# Review From Last Class
- Congress can't abrogate state sovereign immunity through it's article 1 powers (*Seminole Tribe*), but it can do it through Amendment 14, Section 5.
- Why - the 14th amendment is all about limiting federalism and giving Congress the power to limit the states.
## Process for determining valid abrogation.
- Step 1: look for a clear statement of abrogation.
- Step 2: valid exercise of § 5 power (Coleman) - **congruence and proportionality**
	- Identify "injury" - what state violation does congress aim to stop?
	- Identify standard of scrutiny based on this injury - is it rational basis or something else? (Coleman)
		- Rational basis: disability, age, economic policy, pregnancy
		- Heightened: race, sex/gender, fundamental rights
	- Look at the evidence (specifically, evidence Congress had when it passed the law...):
		- For rational basis: we need a "pattern" of state violations - *pattern of irrational conduct* (Kimel, Garrett, Coleman). This is very hard to prove!
			- CANNOT include conduct by local government or private actors.
		- For heightened scrutiny: look at any evidence of state, local, or private violations 
	- Does the evidence support abrogation?

# Testa v. Katt
330 US 386 (1947)
- Emergency Price Control Act - prohibiting sale of cars above a certain price
- Rhode Island Supreme Court refused to enforce the federal law - it's a foreign penal law...
- Legal Q: do state courts have to enforce federal law?
- Holding: RI court has the jurisdiction and **obligation** to enforce valid federal law!
	- **The supremacy clause requires states to enforce federal law!**
- Problem: state courts can have subject matter jurisdiction. So when does a court *have to* enforce federal law?
	- You can't discriminate against federal claims. If a court has limited subject matter jurisdiction, it cannot allow state claims without allowing federal claims over the same issue.

- IRL, these kinds of disputes are pretty rare. State courts like hearing federal claims.

# Alden v. Maine
527 US 706 (1999)
- A group of Maine probation officers sued the state under Fair Labor Standards Act.
- Maine argued sovereign immunity in its own state courts.
- This was the first major state sovereignty case after *Seminole Tribe*.
### Legal Question
- Does a state maintain sovereign immunity, even in its own state courts?
### Holding
- **ARTICLE 1 SAYS STATES HAVE SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY IN STATE COURTS.** There's nothing in Article 1 that says Congress can take sovereign immunity away from state courts. It's no longer Necessary & Proper
	- Concern for state treasuries
	- Unnecessary strain on state political process - if you want the state political process to work, you need to let them decide what to spend money on. You can't do that if the federal government forces them to be subject to certain types of suits.
	- Protecting state dignity - it'd be unfair if states had protection in federal court, but could not protect themselves in state courts.

- There are zero historical quotes about this - the argument is that it was so obvious and beyond the pale that nobody ever argued to the contrary....

- Explaining earlier decisions where it seems like we ignored state sovereign immunity:
	- **Sovereign immunity is a waivable defense. By not raising it, the state is effectively waiving it!!!**

- We presume that states will obey the Constitution and federal laws in good faith. See: Constitution, Article VI, Clause 2.
## Dissent
- We have to adopt a living constitutionalism approach here..
- The state is not sovereign with respect to "the national objective of the FLSA"


## Arguments in favor of no sovereign immunity in state courts
- the constitution doesn't say anything about this
- It would be more dignified for states to litigate these issues in their own court systems - where they have their own judges and determine their own rules - than to do otherwise.

# College Savings Bank v. Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board (*College Savings*)
527 US 666 (1999)
- This is a continuation of a case we discussed last class??
- But the issue is different: it's about some financial product dispute.
## Background
- Trademark Remedy Clarification Act (TRCA) - federal law waiving 11th amendment immunity for misleading advertising from state agencies.
## Legal Q
- Do states "constructively waive" sovereign immunity by engaging in commercial activity?
## Holding
- No.
- *Constructive Waiver* - just by engaging in some activity you're subjecting yourself to suit. The court explicitly does not buy this.
- The text of the 11th amendment makes no distinction between commercial and non-commercial activities.
- Explicitly overturning an older case (*Parden*, 377 US 184 (1964))
## Dissent
- The power to regulate the commercial activity of state actors is rooted in the necessary and proper clause. Otherwise, how can they regulate interstate commerce?
	- **This is not about sovereign immunity - the government is here acting as a private person and thus no longer has sovereign immunity**
- There are areas where the court buys this argument - but not here!!


> [!info] Congress can bribe the states to waive sovereign immunity, but it's hard!
# Sossamon v. Texas
563 US 277 (2011)
**Spending Clause Argument**
- Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act of 2000 - prohibits a substantial burden on the religious exercise of an institutionalized person, absent a compelling state interest, if "the substantial burden is imposed in a program or activity that receives Federal financial assistance." The state also allowed private cause of action, with monetary damages - state is subject to suit for "appropriate relief".
	- RLUIPA was enacted under the spending power!
- Sossamon was a Texas prisoner who sued under the act
## Legal Q
- Is "appropriate relief against a government" sufficiently unambiguous to require state waiver of sovereign immunity?
- In traditional equity cases, it's very common, and much easier, to get damages than injunctive relief.
## Holding
- No.
- "Appropriate relief" is open ended, **too ambiguous** wrt what kinds of relief is necessary.
- Necessary language - "express and unequivocal"
	- Congress has to say "appropriate relief includes monetary damages"
- Repetition of the courts being protective of state coffers - courts have an easier time allowing injunctive relief over monetary damages.
