# Review from Last Class - Taxpayer Standing
- General rule on state and federal taxpayer standing:
	- No standing. It's a purely speculative harm, and there's no causation or redressability.
- But there's a historical exception for municipal taxpayers.
- *Flast* exception (originally created to be very broad, but subsequent courts narrowed it down):
	- Nexus between taxpayer status and type of enactment:
		- Is this about Congress's taxing and spending power (*Flast*, *Valley Forge*)
		- If this is about an action done by the executive, is this a specific appropriation where the statute itself requires the offending executive action, rather than a general appropriation (compare *Bowen* and *Hein*)
		- The decision is legislative - not executive (*Valley Forge*)
	- Nexus between taxpayer status and constitutional infringement:
		- IE, is this alleging an Establishment clause claim.
		- Whether this is actually an Establishment clause claim is about the merits, *not* standing
- Example of precedent where justices don't like the decision but don't have 5 votes to overrule it.

- This doctrine is crucial for Establishment clause cases.
	- The alternative is "offended observer standing" - I was forced to see this and I'm offended by it.
	- Both doctrines are on thin ice under current SCOTUS

- What are allowed injuries that are completely psychic?
	- Aesthetic injury - *Lujan*
	- Injury to conscience - *FDA v. Hippocratic Medicine*
> [!info] There are a lot of areas where the court recognized an "intangible harm" (purely psychic) as a concrete injury

# Third Party Standing
- When a plaintiff brings a challenge on the basis of some third party's rights. They're usually about constitutional rights.
- It's the only type of "prudential standing" rule type that's left. It's a type of case that meets Article III standing but that we don't really like to hear.
- Why do we dislike Third Party Standing?
	- The third party would probably do a better job of representing themselves.
	- Constitutional Avoidance - the court doesn't want to adjudicate a constitutional right unless it absolutely has to. Shouldn't we wait until the third party actually decides brings the claim on their own accord?

## Third Party Standing Test
1. Show typical Article III injury, causation, redressability *for the plaintiff*
2. Close relationship between Third Party and Plaintiff. P relies on T to exercise their constitutional right.
3. Obstacles/Impediments that Third Party would face in adjudicating themselves:
	- Can be legal, procedural, practical, etc.

## Background
- Missouri statute bans abortions that aren't medically necessary from receiving medicate money.
- The plaintiff is the *doctors*
- The third party is patients!
## Legal Question
- Do the doctors receive third party standing?
## Holding
- YES standing
- Do the doctors have Article III standing? Yes
	- Injury-in-fact: Financial injury from not receiving the money
	- Causation: They're not receiving the money bc of the statute.
	- Redressability: If the statute is declared unconstitutional, they'd receive money.
- Big Q: if the third party has Article III standing, why not sue yourself?
	- If all you're asserting is that your property rights have been infringed bc the government doesn't give you money, you'll lose on the merits.
	- But the patient's claim is that their right to bodily autonomy is being asserted here, and that can win on the merits.
- Is there a close relationship between T and P? Yes
	- Doctor-client relationship.
	- The patients rely on the doctor to get an abortion
- Do the patients face some obstacle by bringing suit themselves? Yes
	- They're concerned about their own privacy.

# Craig v. Boren
## Background
- Restriction on selling beer to young men.
- Saloon owner sues over not being able to sell beer.
## Legal Q
- Does the saloon owner have standing for the young men not receiving beer?
## Holding
1. Article III standing? Yes
2. Close relationship? Buyer-seller! That's good enough.
3. Obstacle - they could buy beer if they aged up, so it might become moot over time.
