# Boyle v. United Technologies Corp.
487 U.S. 500 (1988)

- Boyle was a marine helicopter pilot, killed when his helicopter crashed, but he was unable to escape from his helicopter.
- His dad sued the manufacturer of the helicopter based on Virginia product liability law.
- Appeals court overturned this, arguing that this went against "federal common law"
## Legal Question
- Is there such a thing as federal common law, and does it apply here?
- If there's a conflict between federal common law and state law, when do we apply federal common law? IE, when does it preempt state law?
## Holding
- Yes. Defendant wins.
- Test for applying federal common law:
	- There is a "uniquely federal interest" implicated here, and
		- NOTE: when the dispute is purely between private parties, and federal govt. is not affected by the outcome, it's not a uniquely federal interest anymore. Chain of causation is too speculative!
	- There is "significant conflict" between the federal policy or interest and the application of state law.

- Applying here:
	- "uniquely federal interests"
		- federal contracts are a uniquely federal interest
		- civil liability for federal officials for actions take in their course of duty.
	- Significant conflict between federal policy and operative state law
		- Direct conflict between the terms of the contract (create helicopter according to specifications set by the federal government) and state product liability law (create helicopter according to terms that an injured plaintiff claims would be necessary)
		- Federal Tort Claims Act: shields government officials from liability when they perform a discretionary function that is part of their role. Deciding how to design a helicopter is a discretionary function of the engineers involved!
