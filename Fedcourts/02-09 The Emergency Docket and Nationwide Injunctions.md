# On Emergency Orders
- Much of the court's job is to respond to emergency orders to vacate or grant a lower court injunction. These are incredibly influential. But bc of tight deadlines, they're granted without much deliberation, and often with no written opinion.

- Emergency orders are technically temporary, but they often serve as the final word on an issue bc by the time the case can be decided on the merits, it becomes moot.
- Emergency orders are increasingly partisan; with more 6-3 ideological splits over them.
- Often, the court will not issue an opinion explaining how they voted. Increasingly often, there won't be a majority opinion, but you will have a a concurrence or dissent.
	- I wonder if that's contributing to the unpopularity of emergency orders - the public only sees arguments for why they're wrong.
- Common argument: the court is allowed to be less *disciplined* when not writing a majority opinion - it lets them follow precedent more selectively bc they're not adhering to some established doctrine.
- It's unclear if - or how - lower courts should view unexplained emergency orders as binding precedent for future decisions.

- Suggested reforms:
	- Require court to at least give reasons for why they're issuing an emergency order.
	- These explanations should clarify what lower courts are bound by as future precedent.
	- Lower the number of nationwide injunctions issued by lower courts
	- Follow more established principles, and follow them more consistently.


- Some people contend that nationwide injunctions exceed the Article III power of the courts, bc they extend beyond the "case or controversy" before them.
- Others respond that sometimes, complete relief is only possible with a nationwide injunction. Like when doing otherwise would lead to inconsistent enforcement of a right or law.
## Trump v. CASA
606 U.S. __ (2025)
- In January 2025, Trump issued an executive order revoking birthright citizenship for people born on US soil.
### Legal Q
- Does a federal court have the authority to issue a nationwide injunction?
## Holding
- **No**.
- Judiciary Act of 1789 only allowed equitable remedies that already existed in the courts' tradition. Nationwide injunctions did not exist in 1789, so they aren't allowed.
	- **Congress *might* be able to authorize the courts to issue nationwide injunctions!**
	- Point of contention between majority and dissent: British "Bill of Peace." Per majority - these only applied to limited, cohesive groups of individuals, so they're more analogous to modern class actions.
- BUT, the court can still issue injunctions that apply to people not before the court.
- Dissent: how in the world are you supposed to guard against unconstitutional executive orders and protect civil rights without being able to issue nationwide injunctions?
