# 42 U.S.C. § 1983
> Every person who, **under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage, of any State** or Territory, subjects, or causes to be subjected, any citizen of the United States or other person within the jurisdiction thereof to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and laws, shall be liable tot he party injured in an action at law, suit in equity, or other proper proceeding for redress.

History:
- Passed as part of the Ku Klux Act of 1871, under authority of Section 5 of 14th amendment.
- Primarily meant to address constitutional violations by Ku Klux Klan that went unaddressed by local and state authorities during reconstruction.
- For the first ~50 years of its existence, it was basically unenforced. Very few lawsuits under it, and very few successful lawsuits.
	- Part of the reason: the language in it protects "privileges or immunities," which don't matter after the court re-read this phrase in the Slaughterhouse Cases
	- Another reason: the federal judiciary stopped being sympathetic to civil rights enforcement after the end of Reconstruction

- Nowadays, there are tens of thousands of lawsuits under § 1983.
	- What changed this - *Monroe v. Pape*
# Monroe v. Pape
365 US 167 (1961)
## Background
- Plaintiff (Monroe) was heavily mistreated by Chicago police, in violation of due process rights under 14th amendment. He was never charged or criminally prosecuted for anything.
	- Monroe was black

- Without § 1983, how was Monroe supposed to get a remedy?
	- There was no criminal case against him. And even if there was, you can't get this type of remedy in a criminal case
	- You have to have a civil cause of action!

- Brought suit against them using §1983
- Lower courts dismissed complaint, bc the police officers were clearly not acting in accordance with Illinois law.
## Legal Question
- Can §1983 be used when the defendant is violating constitutional rights, but not acting in accordance with any law?
- About what it means for a state official to act "under color of law"
## Holding (Douglas)
- Yes!
- Three purposes of 1983:
	- Override certain state laws
	- Provide a remedy where state law is inadequate
	- **Provide a federal remedy where state remedy was adequate in theory but unavailable in practice.**
- *United States v. Classic*, *Screws v. United States* both set the precedent that acting "under the color of law" includes blatantly misusing power or violating state law, but doing so while "clothed with the authority of state law."

- NOTE: this case also said that, while you can sue Chicago police officers, you cannot sue localities like Chicago itself.
	- Argument from legislative history: they read the rejection of the Sherman Amendment as a rejection of allowing suit of localities.

> [!info] "Under color of state law" is just a state action requirement.
> As long as the offenders are doing so as state actors, engaging in state action.
> Once you establish this, it doesn't matter whether there's a close nexus between their 

## Dissent (Frankfurter)
- The fight between the majority and dissent is about the meaning of the phrase "under the color of state law"

- Dissent's argument: it cannot be used for misconduct of individual state officers, it's about when *the entire state* has made a mistake in its law or custom.
- In this kind of case, where the officer acted in noncompliance with state law, you're supposed to bring a tort claim under state law!
- **§1983 has an exhaustion requirement:** As long as you have a remedy under Illinois state law, somewhere in the system, you do not have a remedy under §1983.

- Raises question: why should we treat state officers differently from private citizens committing the same illegal conduct?
	- State officers are supposed to serve and protect people.
	- There are different penalties for people trying to act in self-defense against cops vs private intruders!

# Gonzaga University v. Doe
536 US 273 (2002)
- Defendants violated FERPA grant requirements by leaking details about plaintiff's (Doe) sexual assault.
	- Doe was a student, wanted to become a teacher. 
	- While Doe was in college, an administrator overheard a bathroom conversation that led her to believe that Doe performed some sexual misconduct.
	- The administrator informed the public school system before informing Doe or initiating any sort of investigation. The public school system refused to give him a teacher certification as a result.
- Plaintiff sued for damages under § 1983 and won in lower courts.

## Legal Question
- Could Doe use § 1983 to gain relief for harm done by violations of FERPA?
## Holding
- No.
- Gonzaga University was a private school - why were they acting under color of state law?
	- They were in cahoots with the public school system!! Informing them in order to help enforce state law.
- **The section only protects individual rights** intentionally created by Congress, not some vague benefits or interests.
> [!important] In standing clause statutes, not only does the rights-creating language have to be clear, but it has to be clear that private suit is their intended enforcement mechanism



> [!info] § 1983 Test (What plaintiff has to show):
> 1. Does the federal statute create a private right?
> 	- Individual, not aggregate focus - statute should say "no person shall ....", (*Gonzaga*, *Talevski*) talk about "rights" (*Gonzaga*, *Medina*)
> 2. Does § 1983 provide a remedy?
> 	- Presumption that there is a remedy!!
> 	- Can be rebutted when there's already a private remedy in another statutory scheme (*City of Palos Verdes*)
- Applying here:
	1. No private right - the statute has an aggregate focus. It talks about the obligations of universities, not the rights of the individuals.
# City of Palos Verdes v. Abrams
544 US 113 (2005)
- Plaintiff brought a case under the Federal Telecommunication Act, arguing that the city violated his rights by denying his zoning case.
## Holding
- Showing that a statute creates an individually enforceable right only creates a rebuttable presumption that there's a § 1983 right.
- The important element here is **Congressional intent**. If you can demonstrate lack of Congressional intent, you can rebut the presumption.

# Health and Hospital Corp. v. Talevski
599 US __ (2023)
- Plaintiff argued that a state-run nursing home violated his rights under the Federal Nursing Home Reform Act
## Holding
- Yes! The right is enforced!
- Going through the test:
1. Private right? Yes. "the *right* to be free from... any physical or chemical restraint imposed for purposes of discipline or convenience and not required to treat *the resident's* medical symptoms."
	- Focus on "the resident" is about showing that this is an individualized right!!
2. §1983 remedy? Yes. The presumption applies, and is not rebutted bc the statute provided no separate remedy.

- Justice Jackson says it's quite hard to pass this Gonzaga-Palos Verdes test! So this is rare and impressive.

> [!info] This opinion also affirms that no laws are excluded from §1983 just bc they're grounded in any specific congressional power (such as spending power)

# Medina v. Planned Parenthood South Atlantic

- South Carolina decided to exclude Planned Parenthood from its list of approved Medicaid Providers.
- Medicaid Act - "any individual eligible for medical assistance ... may obtain such assistance from any \[provider\] qualified to perform the service ... who undertakes to provide \[it\]"
### Holding
- No enforceable right!
1. Private right? No. This language is not clear enough about individual rights
