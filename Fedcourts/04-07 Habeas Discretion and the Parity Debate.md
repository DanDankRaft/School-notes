# Review of last class
- Habeas Corpus is not a review of the conviction. It's a review of the *detention* as being in violation of the constitution, laws, or treaties of the US. We just typically argue that the violation happened as a result of some issue with the trial and conviction process.
- Federal courts started reviewing Habeas petitions in 1867. But in the 90s AEDPA started putting brakes on it.

- To get relief, a federal court must find that the state law:
	- Contrary to federal law (applying the wrong law or ignoring a precedent with materially indistinguishable facts), OR
	- The result of an unreasonable application of federal law
- AND, regardless of the first step, the federal law in question must be "clearly established" by SCOTUS precedent

- On unreasonable application level:
	- Beyond any possibility of fair-minded disagreement.
	- More leeway than "at least one judge found this reasonable."
- On Clearly established precedent:
	- only holding applies, not dicta.
	- The definition of 'holding' for habeas purposes is much narrower than in most contexts.

- Procedural hurdles:
	- Must be in custody/detained: incarcerated or in parole/probation. If you're no longer in detention, the habeas petition is moot!
	- You only have a year. But it's tolled while your state petition is pending.
	- You exhaust all remedies, as defined by state law. Usually, by getting a final judgment on direct appeal, and then getting a final judgment on state habeas (including appeals) before you file a federal habeas petition.
	- You cannot file successive petitions. You can only file one federal habeas petition.

- Why is *Younger* abstention not implicated - there is no longer *pending* state criminal proceeding. And this is not a challenge to the criminal case, it's a challenge to the *detention* - which is a civil process!

# The Parity Debate - Differences Between Federal and State Courts
- Federal habeas operates on the presumption that we do actually need federal courts to intervene in a case where there was already a state criminal proceeding and a state habeas proceeding.
	- But do we?

- Why would we need federal courts when state courts already exist?
	- Competence - federal judges are more competent in general, and especially with state law.
		- Uniformity - have federal law applied consistently!
	- Independence - state judges are elected! They have to run on being Tough on Crime!
		- Federal judges have life tenure and their salaries cannot be cut.
	- Lessens burden on SCOTUS to have to review state habeas petitions!
		- SCOTUS used to have to review all state habeas petition. But as the volume of habeas petitions increased and the number of cases taken has decreased, the court is no longer capable of doing that!
	- Accuracy - the stakes are high and we want a second pair of eyes before we send someone to prison for decades or give them capital punishment.
	- Federal government should check the states!
	- Federal judges are inherently more invested in adjudicating federal law than the states.
- **These are also arguments against jurisdiction stripping!**

- Arguments against federal courts reviewing state court decisions?
	- Federalism concerns - they're intervening in and nullifying state court decisions!
	- Finality - we don't want to make the proceedings even longer
	- Competence - State courts have to apply the constitution all the time!
		- Historical change - for most of the 19th century, federal courts did not have federal general question jurisdiction until 1875! So state courts were the main vehicle applying federal laws!
	- Accountability - state judges are elected! They are more in touch with the community where they have to apply the laws!
		- Elections for state judges were created to state judges *independent* from the appointing governor/legislature!
	- Accuracy - you could have a single federal district judge overturning a ruling of an entire state supreme court sitting en banc!
	- Efficiency - federal courts are already super busy and overburdened! This also goes against finality!

- Parity and Habeas statistics:
	- Being "soft on crime" is bad for re-election.
	- "All judges, even the most punitive, increase their sentences as election nears"
	- Criminal defendants convicted of murder 15% more likely to be sentenced to death on an election year.

# Supreme Court Decisions that grant Discretion

# Burt v. Tiltow
571 US 12 (2013)
Applying the *Strickland* standard.

- What do you do when you bring a federal habeas claim under a broad standard?
- "Double deferential" standard
	- Deference on what the standard actually is
	- Deference on whether the standard was correctly applied
- Under §2254(d)(1), this means you have to prove that the state court applied *Strickland* in an "objectively unreasonable manner"

# Renico v. Lett
559 US 766 (2010)
- Defendant's first trial was a mistrial, resulted in deadlocked jury. Convicted in second trial.
- In state petition for habeas, D argued that the first trial was improperly declared a mistrial.
## Holding
- Petition denied!
- Still "doubly deferential"
- The more broad the rule at issue here, thus the greater likelihood of disagreement among fair-minded judges.
- This is a rule where state judges are owed a ton of discretion, so we are going to give them leeway here.

# Adjudication-on-the-merits When The Court is Silent
- 2254(d) requires deference with respect to any claim that "was adjudicated on the merits in State court proceedings"
# Harrington v. Richter
562 US 86 (2011)
- State court issued an order without an opinion explaining their reasoning.
## Holding
- This is upheld as a valid adjudication on the merits.

- Reasoning: Requiring states to explain *why* they arrived at any decision could undermine the integrity of their case-law tradition. The state supreme court might have too many cases! We can empathize with that (see: shadow docket).

- What you're supposed to do: *imagine* what the reasoning of the state court judgement would have been
	- Later decision: if a *lower* state court said something, you might be able to use that. But if the lower state court decision is "unreasonable," then it's probably not what the state supreme court would've done.
- The burden of imagining is on *the federal judge*.

# Johnson v. Williams
568 U.S. 289 (2013)
- Petitioner raised both state and federal claims, but the court decision against him only issued an opinion that addressed the state claims
## Holding
- This is also a valid adjudication on the merits! If it's fine when they issue no opinion, it's fine if they only issue an opinion on some of the claims.

- Note: if the state court is silent, petitioner can still win by showing there's another case with materially indistinguishable facts. But they can never win on demonstrating unreasonable application
