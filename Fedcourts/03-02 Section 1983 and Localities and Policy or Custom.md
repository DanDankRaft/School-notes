# Monell v. Dept. of Social Services
436 US 658 (1978)
> [!info] Localities can be sued under §1983
> But you have to show *policy or custom* - this is not something present when you're suing an individual officials.

- Pregnant employees at NYC Dept. of Social Services were forced to take medical leave before it was medically necessary. Sued city officials in official capacity under §1983.
- During suit, city changed its policies to ban these forced unpaid leaves.
- Backpay was denied because it would come directly from the city's treasury and would this violate municipal immunity from § 1983.
	- Established in *Monroe v. Pape*
## Legal Qs
- Can municipal governments (or officials acting in their behalf) be sued under §1983?
- Can officials be sued for acting in official capacity but not according to their office's official policy?

## Holding
- Yes and yes.
- *Monroe v. Pape* is overturned.
	- This is extraordinary - especially with how quickly it happened!
- On municipal immunity:
	- They analyzed the the Sherman Amendment, a rejected amendment to the 1871 Civil Rights Act.
	- *Monroe* incorrectly read the debate over it to mean that Congress had no power to impose a civil liability on municipal governments who violate state and federal law.
	- Their reading - they merely could not impose an *obligation* on municipal governments to enforce state law.
	- 
- Subsequent caselaw made it clear that localities are "persons" for the purposes of §1983
	- E.g.: §1983 was used to sue school boards in *Brown v. Board of Education*
- Also, "color of law" means "execution of a government's policy or custom, whether made by its lawmakers or by those whose edicts or acts may fairly be said to represent **official policy**"


> [!impotant] Why introduce this "policy or custom" requirement?
> Courts want it to be the locality's **fault**, not some rogue employee.

- Open Q: do municipal governments get *any* immunity.

# Five Categories of "Policy or Custom"
1. Ordinance or Statute
2. Agency regulation at the local level (*Monell*)
3. High-level official with *policymaking authority* (*Pembaur*)
4. Failure to Train (City of Canton v. Harris) - difficult to establish..
5. Custom (*Monell*)

> [!warning] Avoid taking "failure to supervise" and using it as a category on its own right. That sounds a little too much like respondeat superior

# Pembaur v. City of Cincinnati
475 US 469 (1986)
> [!info] High-level officials with "policymaking authority" create a policy or custom when they issue an order via this policymaking authority
> NOTE: you don't need to demonstrate a pattern! One instance is enough!
> 
- Police officers broke into Pembaur's property to arrest two people who they had a warrant for. They ended up being the wrong people.
	- They did so after consulting with the County Proescutor, who told them to "go in and get the witnesses." The officers subsequently got an axe and chopped down the front door.
	- Prior to this, the county had no on-the-record custom or policy for what to do in this situation.
- A few years later, *Seagald* decision made this procedure unconstitutional and retroactively applicable, so Pembaur sued under §1983.
## Legal Q
- Can the county be liable for the the officers' conduct?
## Holding
- Yes.
- Under Ohio law, the County Prosecutor had the legal authority to establish policy under these circumstances. He was acting as the "final decisionmaker" in accordance with state law.
- Concurrence: municipal liability was only valid because it was not clearly illegal at the time. 
- Dissent: there are factors to test whether a policy was created here for *Monell* purposes, this fails that test.

- Important question: What to do when the order is clearly at odds with the statue or policy used as grounds for §1983?

# Failure to Train as "Official Policy"

# Oklahoma City v. Turtle
471 US 808 (1985)
> [!info] A single incident is not typically sufficient to find that a municipality had some unconstitutional policy
- A rookie police officer shot and killed someone who was clearly not guilty of any crime.
	- Turtle was reaching down to his boot. The officer thought he was reaching down to grab a gun. Turns out it was a toy pistol.. They were in a western-themed bar...
- Victim's widow sued the city and officer.
- Lower courts found in favor of the officer but against the city.
- Legal theory:
	- This incident was proof that the city's "policy of adequate training" was unconstitutional
- Holding: a single incident cannot be enough evidence to hold a municipality liable for having some vague, unconstitutional policy.

# City of Canton v. Harris
489 U.S. 378 (1989)
> [!info] Failure to properly train employees is only valid grounds for §1983 when it comes from a "deliberate indifference" to this type of harm/violation

- Plaintiff was arrested. While in the police station, she collapsed on the floor multiple times but the officers did not summon medical aid. Sued under §1983 for inadequate training.
- Policy: officers authorized to summon medical aid for detainees, at their discretion. But received no training on how to make such determinations.
- Holding: failure to train is only a §1983 violation when "the failure to train amounts to **deliberate indifference** to the rights of persons with whom the police come into contact."
- Case remanded to lower courts to make a finding on this new standard.
# Connick v. Thompson
563 U.S. 51 (2011)
> [!info] Pattern of violations is "ordinarily necessary" to demonstrate deliberate indifference; strictly necessary when dealing with lawyers and not cops.
> We expect lawyers to know the rules because they went to law school and stuff.
> Common response: *people forget what they learned in school!!*
- Plaintiff was wrongfully convicted of robbery bc the prosecution wrongfully withheld exculpatory evidence.
	- The case was about a stolen watch. There was some blood on the watch. A blood test demonstrated that Thompson's blood type didn't match the blood on the watch.
- Plaintiff found out 18 years later and sued locality under §1983.
	- The violation: *failure to train* prosecutors to not withhold exculpatory evidence.
	- Why not sue the prosecutor?
		- He was already dead.
		- He was immune under some doctrine we haven't learned yet.
- Won in lower courts.
## Holding
- Overturned. No evidence of unconstitutional official policy.
- A pattern of violations is "ordinarily necessary" to demonstrate deliberate indiffernce. But not always... cops 
- For single incident: attorneys get a lot of independent training that police officers don't... it's hard to say anything about their actions is the result of a failure of in-house training.
