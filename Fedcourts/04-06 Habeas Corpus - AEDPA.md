# Petition of Habeas Corpus
- Challenge to detention.
- Here, challenge to detention because it violates federal law
- Remedies sought:
	- Release
	- New trial
	- New sentencing hearing
- A habeas corpus petition is a civil action - petitioner v.  respondent.
### History
- Historically, habeas corpus was about *executive detention* - there was a concern that the federal executive would detain people for no reason, throw away the key, and prevent anyone from challenging this.
	- Concern coming out of American Revolution
- So the constitution has a clause saying habeas corpus cannot be suspended except for really extreme circumstances. And people's habeas right cannot be taken away!
- If you a court made a conviction against you, that was enough grounds to say that you were lawfully detained! 
- **Reconstruction** - in 1867, Congress expanded the jurisdiction of the courts to allow federal courts to review state criminal detentions.

# AEDPA
## 28 U.S.C. §2254(d)
- You can't grant a petition for habeas corpus unless detention was:
	- Contrary to federal law, OR
	- The result of an "unreasonable application" of federal law
- And this law was "clearly established" by SCOTUS precedent.

# Williams v. Taylor
529 U.S. 362 (2000)
## Legal Q
- What standard of review does the AEDPA require courts to use?
## Holding (majority)
- "Contrary to" prong:
	1. Wrong legal test, or
		- Ex: not applying Miranda test when you're supposed to.
		- Seems unlikely..
	2. Different results on materially indistinguishable facts.
		- There's a SCOTUS precedent where the facts are similar enough that you just copy their holding.
		- Also unlikely.
- "Unreasonable application" prong:
	- Just being incorrect is not enough!
	- Correct test, unreasonably applied

- Basic principle: it has to be a really, really, really, really, really, really, really, really ,really wrong.
	- From the next case: "Beyond any possibility for fair-minded disagreement"

- Only use binding SCOTUS holdings, not dicta!
## Dissent
- All the AEDPA does is set a "mood" of deference to state courts. If a decision is still wrong, we still get to review it!

# White v. Woodall
572 U.S. 415 (2014)

- Woodall sentenced to death for kidnapping, raping, and murdering a 16yo.
- At the penalty phase, he did not testify. His attorney asked the judge to give a no-adverse-inference instruction from refusal to testify. Judge rejected this request, state supreme court affirmed.
- This case is a petition of habeas corpus over that decision.

- Rule from *Carter*: Because of 5th Amendment, the judge must instruct the jury to not read anything into the fact that the defendant did not testify.
- Rule from another case: trial and sentencing are exactly the same for all 5th Amendment purposes in death penalty cases.

## Legal Question
- Are the courts allowed to grant a petition of habeas corpus here under the AEDPA?
## Holding
- No.

- More information on what doesn't count as "unreasonable application:" 
	- Not extending SCOTUS precedent.

- Clearly established precedent is ONLY HOLDINGS, NOT DICTA.
	- Problem: it's really easy to categorize anything as dicta rather than holding.

 - Problem: 6th Circuit already held that the petitioner is right on this rule.
	 - SCOTUS: **Circuit courts cannot apply their own caselaw. Only SCOTUS precedents count here!**
	 - **Note:** This is different from qualified immunity law. In that realm, both Courts of Appeals and SCOTUS precedent apply! Here, it's only SCOTUS!

- When does the law have to be "clearly established"
	- For qualified immunity: before the defendant acted.
	- For habeas corpus: at the time of the relevant state court decision. You can't expect the state court to know stuff before SCOTUS has said it.



# The full test
- You can't grant a petition for habeas corpus over detention by state court, unless it was:
	- Contrary to federal law:
		- By using the wrong legal test, or
		- You're reaching a different result from a case with materially indistinguishable facts
	- OR, the decision was the result of an "unreasonable application of federal law"

# Procedural Hurdles
1. You have to be in custody! 28 USC § 2254(d)
	- Prison
	- House Arrest
	- Parole or Probation (supervised release)
	- Put in a mental institution - there can be petitions for habeas corpus here, but they're usually not brought through this provision.
	- If you have already been released from detention, you cannot bring a petition for habeas corpus, because **it is moot**.
		- With some exceptions (like having a felony on the record), but those are disputed.
2. Time limit: No later than 1 year after "finality of state court judgment." After the final decision of your final direct appeal.
	1. If you file a state habeas petition, this limit is tolled. The timer pauses until you get the final decision on your state habeas petition!
3. Exhaustion requirement: you can't make a federal habeas petition until you exhaust all of your state options. 
4. Appointed Counsel: Federal court can appoint a lawyer to handle the petition for you. But you cannot complain about ineffective counsel.
5. Successive filings: generally not allowed!
