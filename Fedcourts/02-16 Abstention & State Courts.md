# Review from last class (that i missed :/)
- Abstention - when the federal court has constitutional and statutory power to hear a case, but declines to hear a case anyways.
	- You may hear some constitutional undertones to the arguments, but they're not actually constitutional.

- *Pullman* - kicks in **when there's a state law question and federal constitutional question.** It is a doctrine of constitutional avoidance. state & federal constitutional issue only applies if:
	- state law is unclear; AND
	- state law would resolve case.
- Procedure: stay. It postpones litigation until a state court decides the case. The idea is that if the state courts do not resolve litigation, the parties can still go to the federal court to resolve the case.
- Alternative: certify to state supreme court. If you can directly ask them to answer the question, that's preferrable and even better

- This doctrine became a pain in the butt during the civil rights movement.

- *Younger* abstention - **comes up when there's a pending state criminal prosecution or civil enforcement proceeding**. federal court may NOT enjoin these.
	- Applies even if federal court suit filed first, as long as its still in early stages (*Hicks*)
	- There's an an *anti-injunciton act*. Why didn't the court apply that? It has an exception: a federal court may not enjoin a state proceeding if Congress establishes some exception.
		- One such exception: 42 USC 1983.
- Procedure: dismiss

- Problem: if plaintiffs file the lawsuit too soon, they can't bring it up bc of standing. If they file it too late, they can't enjoin due to *Younger*.

- Abstention doctrine gets abuse of discretionary review at appellate courts (rather than *de novo*). Basically, it's up to the district court to decide whether to grant an abstention.

# Colorado river Water Conservation District v. United States
424 U.S. 800 (1976)

## Background
- Colorado has an elaborate water rights court system. The US sued for water rights in some of CO's state districts, but sued for them in federal court in District 7.
	- They're raising federal law claims.
	- Crucially: sued for using these water rights to support Indian reservations!!

- On the surface, the federal government has jurisdiction over this in federal court. But is there a statute that might revoke jurisdiction here?
	- Maybe: the McCarran Amendment.
	- Federal statute that waives sovereign immunity over water rights, especially under State law.
- It also seems like we're good on constitutional arguments: lawsuits with the US as party fall under Article III.
- Argument for abstention: one of the defendants initiated a lawsuit over the same water right in the state District 7 court, and successfully got a motion to dismiss from the federal district court.

## Legal Question
- 
## Holding
- Abstention order sustained. Case dismissed.

- Typical grounds for abstention:
	- In federal constitutional cases which would be adjudicated very differently under state court.
	- When there are questions of state law that have substantial policy implications, with impact beyond the current suit.
	- In cases involving state criminal, nuisance, or tax collection proceedings.

- *Pullman* abstention doesn't apply because there's no pending federal constitutional issue.
- *Younger* abstention doesn't apply bc there's no pending criminal or civil proceeding under **state** law.

- While its typically fine to have simultaneous state and federal suits over the same incident, sometimes it's unwise to do this.

- Why it's unwise here:
	- Property dispute - water rights are analogous to property rights. But uhh, it doesn't really apply here either?
	- Convenience of federal forum - it's 300 miles away and there are over 1000 defendants.
	- Avoiding piecemeal adjudication - McCarran amendment demonstrates that federal government sets out a policy to avoid piecemeal litigation. Its overall vibes  
	- Order of cases - the federal case was filed first, but there hasn't been a ton of federal litigation 

> [!info] What matters is not the presence of any one (or a combination of these), but of a very strong argument that it'd be judicially wise to move proceeding to state court.

- Here: the McCarran amendment demonstrates a strong federal policy interest in avoiding piecemeal legislation. Litigating everything through CO's dedicated water courts achieves that.
	- Also: federal court is 300 miles away, existing federal litigation in those courts, the relatively sparse proceedings in this federal suit so far.

## Dissent
- The whole exclusive property jurisdiction thing only applies when the subject matter is necessary to effectuate a court's judgment
- This doesn't avoid piecemeal litigation bc the US's claims in District 7 are dissimilar to their existing litigation
- This case is about federal law
- This case is about federal rights of Indian reservations! State courts are not supposed to litigate issues over Indian reservations! That's the whole point of having Indian reservations!

# Rooker-Feldman Doctrine
- Limit based on: 28 USC 1257 - Supreme Court has power to exercise appellate jurisdiction over state supreme courts.
	- *Rooker-Feldman* is an interpretation of this statute. They're reading it to say this means SCOTUS has exclusive jurisdiction over such appeals.
	- Actually unclear whether Congress has the power to grant such jurisdiction
- Applies when:
	- You already lost in state court - judgment has already been entered.
	- Loser brings suit complaining of injuries via state court judgement.
	- They invite federal court to review and reject state court judgement.

- How to avoid Rooker-Feldman?
	- File both lawsuits simultaneously
	- File suit against the actual defendant instead of the state court
	- File under the underlying federal question that provides you a remedy instead of some injury of 



- *Rooker*: party lost in Indiana Supreme Court, filed suit in federal court to challenge the constitutionality of the state court's actions. Since the proper channel was to appeal from Indiana Supreme Court directly to SCOTUS, this was inappropriate.
- *Feldman*: plaintiff lost in District of Columbia Court of Appeals over DC bar admission, sought to review decision in federal district court instead of appealing to SCOTUS. To the extent this was a challenge of the court's decision, and not a challenge over the rules of the DC bar, they should've appealed from DC directly to SCOTUS.

- Underlying principle: only SCOTUS has appellate jurisdiction over a state supreme court. It's kind of like when mom says no and you go to ask dad.
- Where does this come from?
	- 28 USC 1257 - Supreme Court has power to exercise appellate jurisdiction over state court.

# Exxon Mobil v. SABIC
544 U.S. 280 (2005)
- SABIC sued Exxon in Delaware state court, Exxon counter-claimed. Two weeks later, Exxon sued SABIC in federal court over the same controversy.
- Both cases continued, but the state case reached judgment first. Exxon won!
- Federal court proceeding continues. Eventually, the federal case got appealed to SCOTUS.
## Question
- Did the federal court lack jurisdiction due to Rooker-Feldman?
## Holding
- No, but they were (claim and issue) precluded from adjudicating this case further once the state court reached judgment due to the Full Faith And Credit Act, 28 USC 1738.
- Rooker-Feldman only applies in situations where the loser in a state court files an action in federal district court, seeking to overturn the state court's decision.

- Ginsburg's whole point in this opinion was to explain that this doctrine is extremely narrow!
