# Review from Last Class
- Habeas Corpus and procedural default:
	- Court created doctrine, somewhat inferred from the exhaustion doctrine of the AEDPA
	- Procedural default - habeas petitioner failed to present a claim or more claims in state court, in accordance with state procedure. And therefore, cannot make these claims in federal habeas.
- Current rule: When a federal habeas petitioner has failed to present that claim, those claims are barred.
	- EXCEPTIONS:
		- *Wainwright*: "cause and prejudice" test.
			- Cause - petitioner has to have some excuse for not complying with state procedure.
				- Ineffective assistance of counsel - only available in situations where the petitioner is entitled to a 6th amendment right to counsel
				- State interference (e.g., *Brady*)
				- New rules of constitutional law (today's topic)
				- Attorney abandonment (practice problem 35)
			- Prejudice - there has to be a reasonable probability that result would differ absent the federal law violation.
				- This is the same as the prejudice requirement in ineffective assistance claims. SO you can double-dip by just bringing an ineffective assistance claim!
		- Gateway Claims (*House*) - actual innocence claim connected to a constitutional violation.
			- No cause claim - the cause here is actual innocence.
			- Prejudice - extreme standard. But-for the constitutional error, **no reasonable juror** would find the defendant guilty beyond reasonable doubt.
				- They're assuming that this reasonable juror would have access to all admissible evidence at the point of federal review. This also means that the federal court can review the admissibility of evidence!

- Also: *House* says that whatever the standard is for freestanding claim of actual innocence is, it is higher than the standard in House-style gateway claims.

- If there is a procedural default in state court and it goes on direct appeal to SCOTUS via state supreme court, then SCOTUS cannot review it Independent-and-adequate-state-grounds.


- Application: once you get through the procedural default, you have to go through the regular AEDPA analysis. Wait, but problem: the state court never decided this on the merits... so how do we review their case?

# What are "New Rules"
- New rule = **not** "dictated by precedent" apparent to all reasonable jurists.
	- This is very similar to "clearly established law" test in AEDPA! The specifics are a little different, but the broad idea is the same

- Does a new rule apply on federal habeas? **no.**
- Does a new rule apply on direct appeal from state court? **yes.**

- Why shouldn't we apply a new rule on habeas petitions?
	- Would suddenly give tons of prisoners grounds for new petitions. Problems for finality, overwhelming the court system.

- Exceptions (*Teague v. Lane*):
	- New substantive rule.
		- Substantive rule: alters the range of conduct or class of persons the law affects.
		- Ex: *Atkins* - people with intellectual disabilities cannot receive death penalty.
		- Ex: *Lawrence v. Texas* - cannot criminalize sodomy.
	- Watershed procedural rule.
		- Procedural rule: Only affects manner of determining culpability. NOT the range of conduct or class of persons affected.
			- Ex: *Miranda*. Wait, are Miranda rights procedural?

- Before *Edwards*, this framework was established by the case *Teagle v. Lane* (1989)
# Edwards v. Vannoy
593 US __ (2021)
## Background
 - Petitioner was convicted by 11/12 jury members.
 - 2015: He filed a federal habeas petition, arguing that the constitution required a unanimous jury conviction.
 - 2020: *Ramos v. Louisiana* held that the constitution required a unanimous jury for criminal conviction.
	 - Decided while his writ for certiorari was pending in SCOTUS
- Why would we want unanimous juries?
	- Accuracy
	- Racial discrimination
	- In line with the original meaning of the constitution
	- How can you have a conviction "beyond a reasonable doubt" if reasonable jurors disagree?
	- Incorporation - the 6th amendment shouldn't mean something different in federal v. state courts.
	- This precedent is so important that the court in *Ramos* overturned an old decision to establish this! Stare Decisis!
## Legal Question
- Does *Ramos* apply retroactively to overturn his conviction on a federal habeas review of his state case?
## Majority Holding (Kavanauh)
- No.
- **No rule is a watershed rule. This exception is thus abolished!**
- Old test from *Teague*:
	1. Did the case in question (*Ramos*) announce a new rule of criminal procedure?
	2. If it did announce a new rule, is it a "watershed" rule so significant that it should be applied retroactively?
- Kavanaugh: based on subsequent precedent, the watershed requirement is so high that  no rule will ever meet it. This is the most important, bedrock, foundational rule ever and it still doesn't meet the watershed requirements.
- Stare decisis consideration: because of this high bar, nobody reasonably relies on *Teague* to overturn their conviction.
	- Note: he's not engaging with the rest of the typical stare decisis analysis here...
- Conclusion: ***Teague* is overturned**. No new procedural rule applies retroactively in federal review of habeas petitions.
## Dissent (Kagan)
- This is absolutely a watershed rule!
- This rule is so important that, in *Ramos*, it justified overturning a really old precedent!
- Non-unanimous juries were explicitly created as a tool of racial injustice - everyone agrees on that! Even the plurality!
- It makes sense that there are tons of cases that didn't find "watershed" rules - that's what happens when the court creates a requirement that's deliberately narrow by design!
- This goes against stare decisis: nobody here asked us to overturn *Teagle*, and the reasoning here is sloppy.
## Gorsuch Concurrence
- Historically, habeas petitions were only awarded if the convicting state court lacked jurisdiction.
- 19th century Habeas was designed for *executive detention.* If you were convicted in a court, 
- Gorsuch thinks federal collateral habeas review should only be used if the convicting court lacked jurisdiction!!
	- Note: a court might lack jurisdiction if it failed to properly apply a new *substantive* rule of federal constitutional law. If the conviction is outside of what the constitution allows a court to punish, then the court has "no jurisdiction" to punish this conduct!
	- This might mean that Gorsuch thinks AEDPA is unconstitutional.
	- Response: the meaning of "jurisdiction" in the 19th century was much broader than it is in the 21st century. Any unconstitutional sentencing, or sentencing pursuant to an unconstitutional law was considered "beyond the court's jurisdiction."
		- Officially, the only way in which an appellate court can review something is if the lower court "lacked jurisdiction."
## Thomas Concurrence
- AEDPA gets rid of both *Teague* exceptions!
 - AEDPA, by itself, does not allow us to retroactively apply a new federal constitutional rule.
 - Before *Ramos*, Edwards made the jury unanimity claim, but it was rejected on the merits, bc there was SCOTUS precedent clearly against it.
	 - Thus, this decision was "contrary to ... clearly established federal law"
 - What about a successive petition - you can bring a claim about a new rule of constitutional law if that claim was not presented in an earlier habeas petition... Under this prong, any claim that was brought in a prior application, should be dismissed!
