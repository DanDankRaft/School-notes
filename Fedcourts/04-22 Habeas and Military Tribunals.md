# Review from Last Class
- Does Congress have the power to create non-Article III courts? IE, with no tenure and salary protections?
	- Literary interpretation: no.
	- In practice: yes, BUT
### Different theories
(1) Literal reading - there cannot be non-Article III courts.
(2) Limited historical exceptions (*Northern Pipeline*):
- Military courts
- Territorial courts
- Public rights (e.g., government benefits)
(3) Balancing - defer to Congress (*Thomas*, *Schor*). Give them even more jurisdiction when it makes sense!!
(4) It's ok to assign something to a non-Article III court if it's subject to appellate review in an Article III court. Mostly supported by scholars atp.

- Some limits:
	- 7th Amendment: If statute incorporates traditional common law claims, *we can't use an administrative court bc we need a jury* (*Granfinanciera, Jarkesy*)
	- Telltale sign: the relief the agency seeks is NOT an enforcement action that a common law plaintiff wouldn't normally get, IS monetary damages.

- When the government brings a criminal action, it has to be in an Article III court. Always been the case historically.
	- Implication of Jarkesy: if an agency brings an enforcement action that looks like a criminal action, does that require an Article III venue?

# Federal Habeas Review of Federal Executive Detention
- Suspension Clause: "The privilege of the writ of Habeas Corpus shall not be suspended, unless when in cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public safety may require it."
	- Unanswered question: is there a right to federal habeas without any federal statute granting it?
	- Why unanswered? Since 1789, there has always been a statute.
- Why did they care about this? They just had unpleasant experiences with executive detention by the king!!
- How many times has Congress or the President suspended Habeas? 4 times!
	- The Civil War - Lincoln suspended Habeas unilaterally, but Congress ratified it post-hoc.
	- Reconstruction - Habeas suspended in South Carolina to counteract the KKK
	- Philippines - Suspended during a 1905 uprising.
	- Pearl Harbor - Congress suspended Habeas in Hawaii.
- NOT SUSPENDED during Japanese Internment - but FDR's attorney general believed that this made internment illegal.

- Could we suspend Habeas after 9/11?
	- Argument against: we weren't invaded!
	- Argument for: we were invaded by terrorists.

- Question for today: What does it mean for the constitution to apply? When do people get a Constitutional rights?
	- Rights argument: people have inherent rights that always stick with them
	- Territorial argument: the constitution applies within the territory of the United States
	- Limit on government argument: the constitution is a limit on the government. It applies whenever, wherever, and against whomever the government attacks.


# Johnson v. Eisentrager
- German nationals in China were convicted of war crimes by a US military commission.
	- Their crime: continued fighting after Germany surrendered in WWII.
	- Petitioner was one of thousands of similar detainees.
- Since then, they've been deported back to a German prison.
- They challenge their detention in US court.
## Majority Holding (J. Jackson)
- **They cannot file a habeas petition in a US court!**
	- Not US citizens
	- Never set foot in US territory
	- War criminals: unlawful enemy combatants.
		- Lawful enemy combatants: someone fighting the US, but doing so under international laws of war. Wears a uniform, acts under orders from a sovereign government, etc.
		- Unlawful enemy combatants: usually, terrorists. Key distinction: they dress like civilians, and thus put other civilians at risk.
	- Practical concerns: they interfered with the US military! Also it'd be so impractical bc we have to fly everyone out to the US, including the witnesses.
		- Not as important as it is in *Boumediene v. Bush*
## Dissent (J. Black)
- The constitution is a limit on the federal government wherever and whenever it acts.
- The federal judiciary's job is to police what the federal government does!

# Boumediene v. Bush
- Petitioners were arrested by US forces in Afghanistan and held in Guantanamo Bay.
- Justices: we have a lot of history here, but it's not very helpful for us here. Thanks!
## Legal Q
- Can you file a habeas petition if you're a detainee in gitmo?
## Holding
- **Yes you can!**
- Gitmo was US territory for all intents and purposes.
- No practical problems with Gitmo detainees: there were very few people in Gitmo that this applies to.
	- More important here than *Eisentrager*

# Problem 40
Six part test from *Boumediene* for when the suspension clause applies:
1. Citizenship of detainees - are they US citizens?
2. Status of detainees - are they contesting the "charges" they were arrested under?
3. Adequacy of process used to determine their status. Did you face a fair process to demonstrate your innocence when you were detained?
4. Site of their apprehension
5. Site of detention - moreso about the level of US control. Interesting wrinkle: The US military is almost always in control of its bases!
6. Practical obstacles inherent in resolving their petition for writ. 

Source of test - *Boumediene*

- Plaintiffs here are trying to overcome a statute that strips jurisdiction over their habeas petition.
	- Two strategies: find statute unconstitutional, OR read it narrowly.
	- Here: we're trying to show that this statute is unconstitutional here bc it's more similar to *Boumediene* than *Eisentrager*
