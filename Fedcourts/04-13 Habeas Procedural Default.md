# Procedural Default
- Petitioner's claim failed to comply with state procedure. NOT lost on the merits!
	- Why this matters: you need to exhaust state procedures
- Procedural Default - when the state court denies the habeas petition for procedural reasons, rather than on the merits. Usually the result of attorney error rather than strategic choice.
# Daniels v. Allen
- In state court, petitioner's lawyer failed to appeal a claim of racial discrimination in time. He missed the deadline by one day!
	- In direct appeal (of trial, NOT of habeas), if the lawyer missed a deadline, SCOTUS can't review the case because of independent-and-adequate-state-grounds
	- What is adequate and independent state law grounds: SCOTUS interpretation of avoid issuing advisory opinions on direct appeal!
- Holding: *Procedural default* completely barred federal habeas!

- Where do federal courts get jurisdiction over habeas petition?
	- They have statutory jurisdiction over the detention of the prisoner!
	- Independent-and-adequate-state-law-grounds doctrine doesn't apply bc the court has direct jurisdiction here, so their opinions aren't advisory.
	- BUT THE COURT DECIDES TO APPLY IT ANYWAYS.
# Fay v. Noia
372 US 391 (1963)
- Overturned *Daniels v. Allen*
- Three defendants were in cruel and awful detention + interrogation for 27 hours, ended up confessing after basically being tortured into it!
- Two of the three defendants appealed and had their charges dropped. Noia did not.
## Holding
- Procedural default in state court does not foreclose a federal habeas review!
- Exception: "deliberate bypass": when the petitioner intentionally waived a constitutional claim that they're now bringing in a federal habeas petition.

- Context: federal habeas review at the time was done de novo, court was in favor of expanding the jurisdiction of the lower federal courts! They didn't want to have to take direct appeals of state supreme court decisions!
# Wainwright v. Sykes
433 US 72 (1977)
- Petitioner finds a dead person on their porch, tells his wife to call the police.
- Petitioner was informed of his Miranda rights and gave testimony... while drunk.
- Drunk testimony was used to convict him!
- Even though there was evidence that he was drunk, his attorney failed to question the admissibility of his testimony.

- Florida had a "contemporaneous objection rule" - you must object to admission of evidence before trial.
- Federal law - requires a hearing in a state criminal trial before the admission of this type of evidence
## Legal Q
- Did petitioner "waive his constitutional right" and is therefore ineligible for a federal habeas petition?
## Holding
- Yes.

- No longer "deliberate bypass."
	- "deliberate bypass" was dicta!
	- He's worried about petitioner sandbagging - defense counsel intentionally tanking it on state trial, and then brings a last minute constitutional claim in a federal habeas petition!
		- Doesn't make a whole lot of sense! What defense attorney is willing to risk that and put their client in prison for a couple years!
	- Make state trial "the main event." Make sure both parties are bringing their A-game. Witness memories are freshest.
	- If state courts assume that federal court is going to review these cases anyways, they're less motivated to dismiss claims on procedural grounds..
	
- New test: Cause and Prejudice test!
	1. Cause - something independent of habeas petitioner that impeded his ability to comply with state procedure. Non-exhaustive list:
		- Ineffective assistance of counsel.
			- Reasoning: It's the government's job to provide effective assistance. It's a constitutional right! So if attorney is ineffective, that's the state's fault! BUT, only makes sense when you have a 6th amendment right to effective counsel, which is only in trial and first direct appeal.
		- State interference. Things the government does that the petitioner has no way of knowing about.
			- Ex: *Brady* violation (prosecutor withholding exculpatory evidence), jury tampering.
		- Abandonment by attorney - see practice problem!!
		- New constitutional rights (*see* tomorrow!)
	2. Prejudice - Reasonable probability that the result would've been different but-for the constitutional violation!
		- Similar to the *strickland* test. What the hell is the strickland test?
