# Review of Last Class
- The federal government has waived its sovereign immunity in most cases, so we mostly focus on state sovereign immunity
	- Federal sovereign immunity is mostly a matter of statutory interpretation rather than constitutional law.
- Benefits of waiving sovereign immunity:
	- Allows people to enter contracts with them
	- It's politically popular.
	- Enables separation of powers - Congress gets a mechanism that lets them enforce the laws they pass on the executive branch.

	- Literal reading - no foreigner or out-of-stater can sue a US state. But nobody reads it that way
	- Diversity reading - it amends Article III so that diversity jurisdiction no longer exists when a foreigner or out-of-stater sues a state. But there's still diversity jurisdiction when a state is the plaintiff. And there's still federal question jurisdiction!!
		- Within historical context: protects empty state treasuries from common law actions!!
		- Most popular among scholars, still has some popularity among justices
	- Plan of convention - the 11th amendment confirms something that existed before the constitution. A presupposition that a sovereign could not be sued at all. 11th amendment just overrules *Chisholm v. Georgia*.

# Abrogation Under Amendment 14 § 5

## Prologue: *City of Boerne*
- Historically, the court read Congress's power through §5 very broadly, this changed with *City of Boerne*:
	- The court used to read the Free Exercise clause very narrowly. 
	- Religious Freedom Restoration Act: Congress massively expanded religious protection - required cities and states to meet strict scrutiny.
	- *City of Boerne* overturned the Religious Freedom Restoration Act by narrowing §5 of Amendment 14 by creating a test for how to apply it.

# Standards of Scrutiny
- Rational basis:
	- Disability discrimination
	- Age discrimination
	- Economic policy and property rights
		- Incl. wealth discrimination
	- Pregnancy discrimination ...
	- Anything that does not get heightened scrutiny.
- Heightened scrutiny:
	- Race-based classification
	- Sex/gender-based classification
	- Fundamental rights - bill of rights, or the liberty prong of the 14th amendment.
	- A few other limited contexts - not important for this class.

# Coleman v. Court of Appeals of Maryland
566 US 30 (2012)
## Background
- FMLA entitles employees to up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave every year for:
	- (A) the birth of a daughter
	- (B) the adoption of a child
	- (C) the care of a relative with a serious health condition
	- (D) the employee's own serious health condition, when said condition interferes with their ability to work.
- FMLA also allows private right of action for employees **with monetary damages** when their employer refuses to give unpaid leave - including both private and public sector employees.

- Coleman worked for the MD Court of Appeals, sued for failure to grant paid leave under subsection (D).

## Legal Question
- Can Congress waive Maryland's sovereign immunity in this case?
## Holding
- No for subsection (D), but yes for the other subsections.

- Congress is only allowed to waive a state's sovereign immunity for monetary damages under Section 5 of the 14th amendment.

- Test for whether a law is a valid congressional abrogation (Coleman, City of Boerne)
	- Clear statement
	- Proper exercise of §5, 14th amendment.
- Test for whether a law is a proper exercise of this section: **Congruence and proportionality** between (1) the injury and (2) means
	- The injury - a violation of 14th Amendment, §1.
	- The means - abrogation of sovereign immunity.

- **Application here:**
	- **The injury - taking time off when you're sick or injured sounds like disability discrimination! This only gets rational basis, and there's insufficient evidence to back that up.**

## Dissent
- The injury addressed by the FMLA is sex discrimination - there's no reason to say that the self-care provision is somehow completely unrelated to this purpose.

> [!info] The difference between the majority and dissent is how they identify the injury here.


- Reformulates it as 3-part test:
	- Identify provision (Equal Protection)
	- Whether Congress had evidence of a pattern of constitutional violations from the States (yes)
	- Congruence and Proportionality (yes)
- Useful tool for congruence and proportionality argument?: the provision is very limited and modest; doesn't single out any gender, race, etc.

# Identifying the Constitutional Right

## Florida Prepaid Postsecondary Education Expense Board v. College Savings Bank (*Florida Prepaid*)
527 US 627 (1999)
- Background: plaintiff claimed patent infringement against a state agency. Sued under the Patent Remedy Act.
- Holding: abrogation not OK for patent rights.
	- Provision: *procedural* due process claim - no proper venue for a remedy in case of violation of constitutional patent rights by a state.
	- Congressional evidence: none. Did not consider whether states already provided remedies for such patent infringement.
## Kimel v. Florida Board of Regents
528 US 62 (2000)
- About constitutionality of amendment to the Age Discrimination in Employment Act that extended it to public sector employers.
- Holding: abrogation not OK for age discrimination.
	- Provision: Equal Protection - age discrimination
	- The problem: ADEA was based on evidence of a pattern of irrational conduct by private actors - that's irrelevant for state sovereign immunity!

> [!info] In order to identify a constitutional violation in a rational basis area, you must demonstrate a **pattern of irrational conduct** by the state government!
> Evidence by private actors or localities is not enough!
> Evidence of a pattern must be extensive, and stretch across the whole country!
## Board of trustees of the University of Alabama v. Garrett
531 US 356 (2001)
- Repeat of Kimel but with the ADA - employment discrimination.
- Holding: abrogation not OK for disability discrimination here.
- On congressional evidence: evidence of discrimination by local governments not relevant, because only the states are protected by amendment 11.
- Garrett's dissent points out three examples of irrational employment discrimination from state governments. **But that's not enough to establish a pattern**
## Tennessee v. Lane
541 US 509 (2004)
- About sovereign immunity abrogation under Title II of the ADA - which is about public services and programs.
- Plaintiffs were paraplegics who had to attend a state courthouse without elevators. They were arrested because they could not climb stairs to answer criminal charges.
- Holding: **sovereign immunity properly abrogated!!!**
	- The injury - not merely disability discrimination, but due process, and 6th amendment Confrontation Clause - right of access to the courts.



> [!info] Abrogation is almost always ok when the injury receives heightened scrutiny, and almost never OK when the injury receives rational basis.
- This jurisprudence is about separation of powers - it's SCOTUS asserting that it's up to them to determine what the 14th amendment protects.
