# Sovereign Immunity - an Introduction
- Definition: government cannot be sued without its consent. Immunity from both damages AND injunctive relief.
	- In practice, there are a lot of exceptions to the injunctive relief thing.
- Extends to **federal and state governments**, but not local government.
	- Because municipalities used to be corporations!!!

- Sovereign immunity comes from the idea in old England that the king can do no wrong. So why do we still have it?
- **There's nothing in the US constitution about federal sovereign immunity, but we've always assumed that it exists?**
- Why do we have federal sovereign immunity?
	- It would paralyze government if everyone could sue them!
	- "Dignity of government" - customary international law. Countries cannot be sued by each other for wrongdoings, and by private individuals.
- Several statutes waive most of the federal government's sovereign immunity.
	- Administrative procedure act.
	- Federal tort claims act.
	- Tucker act - contract claims against federal government
- Federal immunity today is mostly a matter of statutory interpretation

- Why would you waive your immunity from suit?
	- Political reasons - it's not popular to shield the government from suit. It makes elected officials look bad if the government is immune, for example, from tort suits.
	- Economic reliance - why would you enter a contract with the federal government if you can't enforce it?
	- Separation of powers - it's a way for Congress to ensure that 

# Chisholm v. Georgia
2 US 419 (1793)
- A SC merchant sued the state of GA to recover supplies from some contract.
	- Fun fact: the plaintiff's lawyer was the AG at the time. This is bc the AG job sucked so much back then that he had to take on private clients.
- SCOTUS found that they had jurisdiction to adjudicate this, despite GA's sovereign immunity.
	- Article III, section 2 includes a lot of language about suits between states and people. They read that as eliminating states' sovereign immunity.
- This generated so much backlash that it led to the passage of amendment XI.
	- Georgia also said "if you ever do that again we will kill you"
	- This was one of the fastest ever turnouts for a constitutional amendment.
	- No concern about federal question jurisdiction bc federal courts didn't have it until 1875!
# Amendment XI
> The Judicial power of the United States shall not be construed to extend to any suit in law or equity, commenced or prosecuted against one of the United States by Citizens of another State, or by Citizens or Subjects of any Foreign State.

### Article III, Section 2
> The judicial power shall extend to all Cases ... arising under \[federal law] ... to Controversies between two or more states; between a state and citizens of another states; ==between a State & citizens of another state==, between Citizens of different states ... & between a State, or the Citizens thereof, & foreign States, Citizens or Subjects.
# Three Theories of State Sovereign Immunity
1. literal reading - cannot sue a state by any out-of-stater or foreigner.
	- Almost nobody reads it this way...
2. diversity theory - you need to read it as a modification to Article III.
	- Amendment XI modifies [[02-17 State Sovereign Immunity#Article III, Section 2]] to say that federal courts no longer have **diversity jurisdiction** over lawsuits between a state and citizens of another state, or a state and a foreign citizen, so long as the state is the one being sued.
	- This is the dissent's idea in *Seminole Tribe*
3. Plan of convention - Sovereign immunity for the states is *presupposed by history*. Even for federal questions!
	- Amendment XI was supposed to correct for the evil of *Chisholm v. Georgia* by clarifying what the founders always meant with state sovereign immunity.
		- Counter-argument: Amendment XI goes further! It includes a new clause for foreign citizens! And if they wanted complete sovereign immunity, they could've ratified a different proposal. But they explicitly rejected that!
	- Actual scope:
		- Plaintiffs: in-staters & out-of-staters & foreign governments. **BUT NOT THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT** (See United States v. Texas (1892) - a state waives immunity from suit by the federal government when it's admitted to the union)
		- Subject Matter: federal question & diversity
	- Congressional abrogation:
		- Requires a clear statement of intent to abrogate immunity
		- Authority to abrogate CANNOT COME from Article I. Must come from Amendment XIV
	- This is the majority's idea in *Seminole Tribe*

# Ways around state sovereign immunity
- Federal government can sue a state
	- Can states sue the federal government? Yes through statutory waivers.
- Abrogation under Section 5, Amendment XIV
	- Clear statement
	- Is Congress actually using its Section 5 power
- State consents or waives immunity!
	- Federal government can induce this with money!!
- Sue state official for prospective relief

# Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida
517 US 44 (1996)
## Background
- Indian Gaming Regulatory Act - imposes a duty to negotiate in good faith on states to allow Indian reservations to establish gambling institutions, including a clause making it judicially enforceable by allowing Indian tribes to sue in federal court for a state failing to negotiate in good faith.
	- Based on the Indian Commerce Clause of the US constitution.

- At the time this litigation started, nobody argued for sovereign immunity. Nobody thought this was a serious argument until appeals.

- On the one hand, it looks like personal jurisdiction bc **it's a waivable defense**.
- On the other hand, it looks like subject matter jurisdiction bc **you can bring it up at any point in the case**.

- For the purposes of this class, it's just a strong affirmative defense for a state.
## Legal Question
- Is the enforcement clause constitutional? Or is it a violation of state sovereign immunity (Amendment XI)
## Holding (Rehnquist, Scalia, Thomas)
- No. The clause violates state sovereign immunity.
- Reading Amendment XI broadly - each state is sovereign, and sovereignty means individuals cannot sue them without their consent.
	- Even though it seems to say something narrow, we've read it to mean the penumbras and enumerations of Amendment XI.
	- A literal reading of Amendment XI is a strawman.
- Test for whether Congress violated Amendment XI:
	- Did it unequivocally express its intent to abrogate the immunity
	- Has it acted pursuant to a valid exercise of power?

- Application here:
	1. Yes, it did express intent to abrogate immunity.
	2. The only valid way to abrogate immunity is on 14th amendment grounds (see combination of clause 1 and clause 5 of that amendment)
- In reaching this decision, they explicitly overturn *Union Gas*, a semi-recent plurality decision that allowed the Commerce Clause to abrogate 11th amendment.
## Dissent
- Argues for the *diversity theory*.
