# Defining Copyright
- Copyright is a sub-field of intellectual property, which is a sub-field of property.

- What is property?
	- Layman's theory - Exclusive rights of people in objects. A legal relation between objects and people.
	- Better theory - A relationship between people. Property rights do not exist in a vacuum. There must be at least two people involved, each with different rights! It's more similar to contracts or torts.
	- **Property is a legal relation between people with respect to external resources in the world**
		- Resources - objects that have a use value.
- What is *intellectual* property?
	- Property rights wrt to *intangible* resources. IE, information.
	- What makes IP unique from other types of property relations?
		- It relates to **public goods** - which are non-excludable and non-rival.
		- Rivality and exclusivity are both spectrums - they're not absolutes.
	- Rivalry: Intellectual Property Rights take non-rivalrous goods and introduce rivalry to them!
	- Exclusivity: Non-tangible resources don't have tangible boundaries. There are no physical boundaries that demarcate where the property begins or ends.
		- Ex: if you copy someone else's novel, how much of it do you have to modify before you violate copyright?
- **Copyright specifically protects forms of human expression.** As opposed to patents which protect inventions or trademarks which protect brand identity.
	- Copyright act calls it "works of authorship"
### Unique qualities of human expression (compared to other types of IP)
- Standing on the Shoulders of Giants - all human expression which is protected by copyright borrows elements from other works of human expression which are protected by copyright. We don't express things in a vacuum! There's a kind of catch-22 here!
- Creativity - *unlike patents,* human expression does not have to be useful.
- Speech - the "human expression" that copyright covers is the same as "speech" covered by freedom of speech. Problem: copyright is all about preventing others from expressing this speech!

- Threshhold requiremenents of copyright:
	- Fixation - has to be expressed outside of someone's head
	- Originality
	- third thing

# Open Source Yoga Unity v. Choudhury (N.D.Cal 2005)
- Choudhury has copyrighted a specific sequence of 26 yoga poses ("asanas"), but not any of the individual poses.
- He is seeking to enforce his copyright against other yoga studios teaching this sequence.
## Legal Qs
1. Is the yoga sequence copyrightable?
2. If so, is OSYU infringing?
## Discussion
### Copyrightability
- Rightsholder wins. No SJ on this question.
- Two competing copyright principles:
	- Copyright does not protect factual or functional information
	- Copyright extends protection to a sufficiently creative arrangement of public domain information.

- Eg, alphabetical listing of businesses and their contact info - NOT copyrightable
- listing of businesses of particular interest to the chinese community - copyrightable.
- Fixed routines are copyrightable, but unscripted activities are not.
- This is a very narrow copyright
#### Class Discussion
- Maybe the court's decision to adopt a narrow copyright is OK.
- Can you copyright a sequence of things you didn't create?
- Fundamental subject matter questions - a martial art is not the kind of thing that copyright should protect! Also, Choudhury keeps saying this sequence is valuable because it is useful!! Utilitarian benefits v. aesthetics.
- unscripted or non-fixed routines - e.g., sports moves - are not copyrightable. They don't have a fixed structure.
- Should this be copyrighted?
	- If this wasn't copyrighted, would Choudhury still be incentivized to create this sequence? 
	- Yoga instructors don't strike me like the kind of people that need a profit incentive to create new yoga sequences.
	- People can create things due to intrinsic motivations too!
	- People can even make a profit off of their creations through a **first mover advantage** - without even having to exclude others from this work
	- Is it good for the field to introduce a profit incentive here?
### Infringement
- Rightsholder wins. No SJ on this question.
- Exact duplications of the sequence would constitute infringement.
- "Substantially similar" routines can constitute infringement, but it's unclear if that's why OSYU and its instructors teach.

# Levola Hengelo BV v. Smilde Foods BV (CJEU)
- Heksenkaas - a cream cheese and fresh herbs dip
- In 2011, its creator transferred all intellectual property rights over it to Levola
- Levola seeks to enforce its copyright over the dip's **taste**
- LEGAL CONTEXT: this case is about interpretation of EU directives and the **Berne Convention** on copyright - **to which the US is a signatory!**
## Legal Q
- Is taste copyrightable under the Berne Convention?
## Discussion
- Rightsholder loses - taste is not copyrightable.
- Taste doesn't have "objective structure" - a requirement of copyright
	- Taste is all about the subjective experience of the taster.
	- Do we agree with this?
		- Taste is not measurable. There is no scientific way of determining whether two products taste the same.
		- Even if the chemical structure itself is the same, two people might still perceive the taste differently.
		- If we're trying to protect the chemical composition - that's covered by patent.
- Bracha: taste is more of a *function* of a certain food rather than a work of authorship.

# Economic Analysis of Copyright
- If we want to make a normative/policy argument about copyright, we have to understand the economic mechanics of how copyright works.
- Intellectual Products are "Public Goods"
	- Non-excludable: it's impossible to exclude others from using them (without legal mechanisms).
	- Non-Rivalrous: one's enjoyment of an intellectual product does not diminish another's enjoyment of it.
- Aren't public goods awesome???

- In order to have any use of rival resources, we need a **governance mechanism** (like private property rights) that decides who gets exclusivity rights over certain goods and how they can use it.
	- Otherwise, nobody will derive any use of the rival good because it will be shared between too many people.
- Without rivality, the **governance mechanism** becomes unnecessary

- So why do we still have copyright? It allows you to **profit** off of your own creation.
	- **The incentive function of copyright**
## The Incentive Function of Copyright
- Intellectual goods are created by humans and take a lot of work.
- Without the ability to exclude others, it gets very hard to profit off of one's creation, which makes it difficult to incentivize people to produce it.
- The cost of innovation is high, while the cost of imitation is low.
	- Why would I create anything when I can make much more money by copying others?
	- Why would I create anything if others will copy me, and it'd be nearly impossible for me to profit off of it?
- "Appropriability problem": There is a gap between the value of a good to society and the value a creator gets to recoup. 
- What the demand curve teaches us:
	- Without copyright, competition will drive down the cost of a good to its marginal cost (the cost to print another copy).
	- Many people will buy it at that cost, but 
## How to Solve the Public-Goods Problem?
- Government provides the good
	- Ex: the military, roads
- Government subsidizes production
	- Ex: public research, National Endowment for the Arts
- Government issues prizes
	- Ex: reward system for atomic-energy inventions? What is this referring to.
- Government assists private parties in increasing excludability
	- Ex: trade secrets, anti-circumvention (e.g. DMCA) laws
- Government grants a legal **right to exclude** (ie, property rights), conferring some pricing power on producers.
	- Ex: **Copyright**

- How does the right to exclude solve the appropriability problem?
	- It grants me the legal power to reduce competition enough that I can sell copies of my work above the marginal cost, and thus profit from creation itself.
- Copyright is kind of a compromise: it solves the public goods problem by increasing prices for consumers and eliminating some competition.
	- It eliminates competition for different reproductions of the same good
	- It does NOT eliminate competition between different, similar intellectual goods.

# Downsides of Copyright
### Static Cost of Copyright - cost for consumers
- You're decreasing access and consumption by increasing prices!
	- Why? Because increasing prices is an essential component of how copyright works!
	- Copyright is "a tax on readers" - Lord Babington
	- Also called *deadweight loss* - the marginal cost of letting additional people consume the good is higher than the marginal benefit to society.
- Price discrimination: A producer can charge each consumer exactly how much they're willing to pay. In reality, that's rarely possible.
	- Partial schemes of price discrimination: hardcover vs soft-cover
- Much more feasible - one price for everyone. Find the profit-maximizing equilibrium price based on the supply and demand curves.
	- The effect of this:
		- Some consumers will pay less for the good than they're willing to pay
		- Other consumers who would value the good won't get it! Every consumer falling here is *deadweight loss*
### Dynamic Cost of Copyright - cost for creators
- Copyright suppresses certain kinds of creative production!
- There are a lot of socially valuable works that never got created bc they required licensing from someone else! And the cost of licensing was higher than the value of the work
	- Ex: a documentary using someone else's footage
	- Ex: a work using someone else's characters

> [!info] Takeaway
> More copyright is not always better. We want to reach a balance that maximizes the utility of copyright
- Example of balancing: we let copyright expire!
	- Other forms of IP (trademarks, trade secrets) don't work like this!
- What if we could fine-tune the duration of copyright for each individual work? Find the minimum duration that allows me to recoup production costs and not a day more.
		- But like all modern law, we can't fine-tune copyright for each work, we have a uniform law that applies equally to all work.
- By applying uniform copyright protections to all work, we have to play a balancing exercise, because some works will not get enough protections and some works will get too many protections!!

# 17 U.S.C. § 102
>(a) Copyright protection subsists, in accordance with this title, in *original works of authorship* . . . Works of authorship include the following categories:
	(1) literary works;
	(2) musical works, including any accompanying words;
	(3) dramatic works, including any accompanying music;
	(4) pantomimes and choreographic works;
	(5) pictorial, graphic, and sculptural works;
	(6) motion pictures and other audiovisual works;
	(7) sound recordings; and
	(8) architectural works.
- **This list is non-exhaustive!!**
(b) In no case does copyright protection for an original work of authorship extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work.

- Copyright protection extends to **original works of authorship** in **any tangible medium of expression** from which they can be **perceived, reproduced, or otherwise communicated**
- It does not extend to the underlying **ideas, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery**

# Bracha & Syed - Beyond the Incentive-Access Paradigm
- Information goods are **public goods** - nonexcludible and nonrival.
	- Nonexclusive: it's difficult to grant access to the good to some but not others.
	- Nonrival: one person's consumption of the good does not erode others' ability to consume it.
- Property rights introduce exclusivity.
- Why would you want exclusivity?
	- With a nonexcludible good, if the cost of innovation is higher than the cost of imitation, then it is not profitable to create new goods.
- Why does it suck?
	- Introducing an exclusivity mechanism to a nonrival good creates **deadweight loss**. The cost of access is higher than it would've been for a work in the public domain.
- Copyright incentivizes the development of new works by letting rightsholders charge more for their good than is strictly necessary to recoup the cost of development and reproduction. This enables them to profit off of creation, at the expense of creating deadweight loss.
- Copyright creates deadweight loss *across* innovations, not *internally* to any individual innovation.
	- It incentivizes innovation where the cost of innovation is low and the profit for reproduction is high. These works exhibit *more* deadweight loss than necessary. It disincentivizes works where the cost of creation is high and the profit for innovation is low. These works exhibit *less* deadweight loss than necessary.
	- Note: these incentives do not cleanly map to promoting socially valuable innovations!
